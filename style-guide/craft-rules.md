# Craft / Writing Rules (Non-Negotiable)

These are the hard rules. Nothing in `style-guide/voice-technique.md` or anywhere else overrides them.

- No em dashes, anywhere, ever.
- **Before adding anything, check it against `outline/canon-ledger.md` and the chapters; if it contradicts another item, do not add it, mark it OPEN.**
- **Inner monologue (first person): quotation marks AND italics (the author's format, final, do not change it).** The narration is first person, past tense, inside Tatsuya's head, and his direct thoughts are set off as `*"Damn, she has me cornered."*`, short and in his own voice, present tense inside the marks, with a tag such as "I thought" only when needed. Plain narration stays narration. Spoken dialogue is quotation marks without italics. This replaces the plain-narration rule I wrongly introduced in the first-person conversion. Full notes: `style-guide/first-person-voice.md`.
- **Text messages, notes, and other on-screen/written text get square brackets, plain, not bold, not quotation marks.** `[Like this.]` No bold wrapping. Keeps them visually distinct from spoken dialogue and from inner monologue at a glance, the same instinct as the blue-box UI presentation for game text (see `codex/lore/deck-building-rarity-and-classes.md`), applied to real-world written text too. OPEN: Chapter 1 sets texts and Dad's letter in bold brackets, while this rule and Chapter 8 use plain brackets; not decided.
- One speaker or thinker per paragraph.
- **No isolated one-sentence paragraphs for false emphasis.** A short sentence standing alone as its own paragraph reads as AI-generated rhythm, not deliberate style. "Show don't tell" is not license for choppy one-liners, prose should read as full, connected paragraphs the way the rest of the finished chapters do. A single short sentence can still end a paragraph for a real landing beat, it just can't stand alone as the whole paragraph.
- Full sentences in narration, no action fragments.
- Every line of dialogue paired with an action, posture, or tone cue. No floating quotes.
- **Every dialogue beat needs two real sentences, not one.** One line of dialogue is not a complete beat on its own. It needs a paired sentence carrying a real, specific, visually-observable action, posture, or tone cue, something the POV character could actually see or hear the other person doing, not an internal state stated flatly ("she was nervous"). A bare `"Dialogue," X said.` with no cue attached is a floating quote and doesn't satisfy this rule even though it technically has a dialogue tag. This doesn't mean padding every exchange into paragraphs, in a fast back-and-forth the cue can be as small as a glance, a head tilt, a shift in weight, but it has to be there. See `style-guide/voice-technique.md`'s parallel rule for inner-monologue micro body cues, they're the same discipline applied to two different beat types.
- **NPCs never express naive surprise about their own species' place in this world.** Any character whose race has an established social standing (goblins and other demi-humans read as monsters by human kingdoms, per Shiv's own account in Chapter 7) already knows and has always known that about themselves, it's lived reality, not news. They can be bitter, resigned, defiant, or flatly matter-of-fact about it, but never confused or surprised by it, "wait, aren't we not monsters?" is a modern person's reaction dropped into a body that's lived this its whole life, and it breaks immersion every time.
- **POV:** First person, past tense, locked inside Tatsuya's head at all times, no exceptions. The narrator never takes over, everything the reader learns about the world, other characters, or the setting has to arrive through what I see, hear, do, or think, not through outside exposition. Nothing I cannot perceive is narrated: other characters' feelings arrive as what I read in their face, voice and posture. Real world first, VR second. No generic fantasy narration inside HGO. Real-life pressure (bills, twins, mom, Tabuchi) never fully turns off, even in-game.
- **Return to the body after information.** After any tactical, mechanical or information-heavy passage (roughly every two or three paragraphs of analysis, system text, exposition, or another character explaining something), bring Tatsuya back into his body with a small specific cue, not only after dialogue: rubbing his eyes, squinting at the fluorescent light, a wince, a loc falling or a hand raked through his hair, a brow or jaw change, hands flat on a surface, a shift of weight, a breath. In-game, use the goblin body (cold, claws, smaller frame). Vary the cues and do not repeat one more than a couple of times in a chapter. Check `style-guide/physical-expression-thesaurus.md` first.
- **Chapter target:** roughly 3,000–4,000 words (updated from the earlier 2,000–2,500 baseline as the author's actual chapters settled at this length). Serial format. Every chapter ends on a decision, complication, reveal, or gut-punch. No neat bows. No recap-heavy internal monologue. No lore dumps, the world is revealed only through Tatsuya living inside it.
- **Real-world elapsed time, revealed indirectly, from Chapter 9 forward.** Prefer a character noticing or reacting to how much time has actually passed (checking a phone, surprise at the gap) over narrating a precise countdown ("in ten minutes," "twenty minutes later"). Keeps real-world time feeling like it's moving faster than the reader tracking it minute-by-minute would suggest. Not retroactive, already-drafted chapters (through Chapter 8, and Chapter 9's existing draft) keep their existing precise time callouts unless a chapter is otherwise being revised anyway.
- **Combat stats, from Chapter 9 forward:** once a real fight is live, damage output and kills/deaths/assists surface as real numbers, not just qualitative tags, see `codex/lore/five-disciplines-stat-system.md`'s revised house-style note. Not retroactive.

## Open Questions / Flags for Confirmation

1. **Companion/business name:** one dictated note referenced a company name that sounded like "Kazuto Tech" in the middle of a sentence about the pod exchange program. This assumes that was a slip for Kazuku/Nexus Enterprises (the only publisher/hardware entity established in canon) rather than a fourth company. Flag if that's wrong.
2. **Pass-out limit vs. 72-hour death lock:** the pass-out lock defaults to 24 hours, shorter than the flat 72-hour death lock. The 24 hours is a provisional default and needs confirmation before either is dramatized on the page. See `codex/lore/preserved-misc-mechanics.md`.
- **Concrete detail, sparing figures.** Keep the reader in Tatsuya's physical experience; cut abstract labels, borrowed metaphors, stacked comparisons and filter words where the plain fact can stand. Limits that protect the author's voice: `style-guide/concrete-detail-and-figurative-language.md`.
- **Speech continuity.** A speaker's consecutive beats connect. The next beat picks up the last one ("But look at who they're marching for") and never restates it or resets with a fresh opener. Say a fact once.
- **Exposition earns its place by cause and effect.** Every piece of game or world information must lead to the next beat (the sponsor puts the finale on the screen, the finale sets up the announcement, the announcement is how Tatsuya learns of the sale and the prize). A pregame summary followed by a tactical read is an intended broadcast structure, not a dump; keep it short and move on.
- **Names and places must make sense on the page.** A city is not "in the plains." Check that a place name matches what the place is before it is used.
- **Commentators sell, they do not know.** In-world announcers read the story as players, state only public facts, cite what they have read (Loop, the HGO forums) and give no spoilers.
- **Balanced word choice (author-stated).** Do not use words normal readers cannot understand or that read like a name ("christen"). Do not flatten everything into simple words either: keep the story's natural, varied vocabulary and its voice, and swap a word only when it would trip the reader up. Game and tech terms the story itself teaches are fine.
- **Distinct voices (author-stated).** Every character sounds different. Tatsuya is a hype, chaotic good type with dry humor and real emotion, never monotone; he holds his composure but stands up for himself, and when he feels empowered he turns cocky and entitled. Eren is an absolute jackass. Haruto is a jock, easygoing and careless about other people. Do not give two characters the same voice.

## Emotion fits the scene

Every beat carries the feeling that scene calls for, and it changes as the scene turns. Waking late is panic, a note that explains everything is relief, a text that says the drive is ready is purpose. Do not run one flat register ("What the hell", then a shrug) across a scene that moves. For each scene, name the emotion on the way in, the one that turns it, and the one he leaves with, and show each through his thoughts, body and what he does next. Check this before the concrete-detail pass, and keep it in his voice (a drip of humor is fine, never a replacement for the feeling).

## Show, don't tell (ideas and plans)

Tatsuya does not explain a business idea, a mechanic's loophole or a plan to the reader. An idea lands harder when another character hands him a piece of it and he puts it together on the page, so the reader solves it with him. Keep his own lines to a short reaction ("that tax eats most of it") and let the other person's story carry the information. If a paragraph reads like a lore explainer in his voice, cut it back to what he sees and says, and move the information into someone else's mouth or a later scene.

## No metagaming, for every character

A character only knows what they saw, heard, were told, or can reasonably look up. If they were not in the scene and had no eyes, ears or source on it, they do not have the information, and that includes Tatsuya. Before a character acts on, mentions or reacts to a fact, name where they got it. Gato was not there when the scooter died, so he cannot mention it. If a character needs the fact, give them a source on the page (a text, a witness, a post they could read) or cut it.


## No self-explanation after a beat

When a beat lands, move on. Do not follow it with a sentence that restates what just happened or explains why he did it ("He wasn't mad. He'd called it before I could... I owed him one"). The reader sees it. Give a short human reaction (*"Damn. He ain't tripping."*) and go to the next thing. If a motive matters, hint it with a detail (extra hours, extra money; paying what Chiyo asks and knowing it is not the real number) and trust the reader. Explain only when the information is new and the reader could not get it from the scene.


## A revision replaces, it does not add

When a chapter is revised, new beats replace or fold into the lines already there. A revised chapter should not end up longer than its source unless the author asks. Add a beat only by cutting the sentence it makes redundant. Check with `python3 tools/chapter_check.py <new> --source <old>`.


## Use what you bring up

Do not mention a sensory detail or object you do not use. If the fridge hums, he goes to the fridge. If it is only noise, tie it to something he does (city sounds reach him on the couch before he gets up). Every detail either gets used or gets cut.

## Do not overexplain; he learns on the page

Do not explain systems or numbers up front. His advantage is his game knowledge, and he learns the troop system, the guild numbers and the rest by playing. Give the reader the same moment of discovery.


## He does not have all the answers

In first person, Tatsuya's inner talk shows how he approaches things, not a finished answer. Where he lacks information (people's motives, Dad's death, the bear, whether something will work, what the game does) he guesses, hedges, worries or doubts, and he can be wrong. Use plain hedges sparingly ("probably", "I figured", "as far as I could tell", "maybe") and vary the feeling: curiosity, worry, doubt, hope. Where he is an expert (his tech work) he stays confident. Doubt always comes with a move: a guess, a test or a decision, so the scene keeps its pull. Do not turn author lore into his confident statements.

## Moving through the world (author-stated)

- No teleporting. Every move to a new place gets a short transitional beat: he travels, and the world moves around him. One line is enough, not a paragraph of scenery.
- A new location gets its tone set once, for him, through the senses that matter there (sight, sound, smell, touch, temperature). Keep it short and immersive, not an inventory.
- After that, bring the environment back only when something changes (a blackout, an attack, a building damaged) or when he is about to leave. Do not repeat it for its own sake.
- Immersion is the goal. Cap the extra detail so travel does not drag.
