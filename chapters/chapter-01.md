# Chapter One: The Countdown Starts

Nobody left at WattsUp this late but me. I worked a rag along the counter in slow, even strokes, cleaning that filled time more than it filled a need.

The counter had been clean for an hour already. Outside, the canopy lights hummed over three empty charge bays, the cables hanging slack from their housings, the tall pylon sign out front cycling through ad after ad against a sky gone fully dark. Past the old gas pumps standing dark at the far end of the lot and the string of little flags along the canopy's edge, Shibuya kept doing what Shibuya did at midnight, glowing like it had somewhere better to be.

An electric sedan had pulled into the middle bay a little after eleven, a man in a rumpled suit behind the wheel thumbing his phone while it took its charge with a soft double chime. He never came inside. Nobody did, anymore. The old gas pumps stood dark with their hoses coiled and dusty, and I couldn't remember the last time anyone had pulled up to one. The motor cars were fading out of Japan the way film had, and WattsUp had been quick to rip out the signs and call itself a charging chain. The last person I'd known to keep feeding one was Dad, whose hybrid still drank gas, hand controls worn smooth under his palms, and I pushed that thought back where it lived and went back to the counter. I squinted against the fluorescent tubes buzzing overhead and rolled my neck until something popped.

The register drawer ticked in the quiet, a number I could have recited without looking. Gato was a good boss and the job was steady, and steady covered rent and groceries and not much else. I tried not to do the other math.

I caught myself in the dark half of the window between the counter and the door and looked longer than I meant to. Locs pulled back off my face with an elastic gone soft from reuse, a few loose ones falling free at my temple no matter how many times I tucked them back. The scar over my right brow, thin and pale, catching the fluorescent light at an angle that made it look almost fresh even eleven years out from the car accident that put it there. Dark skin gone sallow under the store lighting, the kind of tired that lived behind the eyes more than anywhere else. I had my mother's height and my father's hands, steady and precise, the kind that fixed things instead of breaking them, and some nights working nights like this that felt like its own kind of inheritance I wasn't sure I'd earned yet.

My phone buzzed against the counter, the vibration loud enough to cut through the hum of the store. Chiyo's name lit up the screen.

**[Tabuchi called again. Told them the usual. The lawyers are handling it, so don't worry if anything turns up at the hotel, I've got it. Also, Tochi's application window opens back up Jan 4, I'm gonna need a response soon, Tatsu.]**

I read it twice, once for the words and once for the weight sitting right behind them that she hadn't bothered spelling out. Chiyo called it ordinary debt collection, same as always, and mentioned the lawyers like it was nothing. I'd stopped being sure it was ordinary a while ago. The demands kept circling back to my father's old paperwork specifically, in a way that never fully squared with "just pay us."

Dad wouldn't want her fighting this battle for him. The thought settled hard and quiet in my chest. This wasn't hers to carry. Never was.

I typed back a thumbs-up emoji, because a thumbs-up cost nothing and didn't invite follow-up questions I didn't have answers for yet, and flipped my phone face-down on the counter.

The screen mounted over the register ran what it always ran, HGO, and I hadn't picked it. All day it had sat at the edge of my eye, a bright rectangle I'd trained myself not to look at, the same logo, the same clips, the same voices selling the same thing. The WattsUp Corporation had bought a sponsorship slot on the game, the same as a dozen other small companies trying to get their names onto one of the biggest titles out there while it was still locked in closed beta, and the company's stock had climbed with the game every week since. So corporate had every screen in every store looping it. Gato had stopped complaining about it a month ago. Most nights that meant some replay or guild footage playing quietly behind me while I worked, and I kept my back to it. I'd gotten good at that. Tonight corporate had turned the volume up on the whole chain for the last battle of the closed beta, the one they'd been pushing all week, and it was getting harder to talk myself out of listening.

Then Yuna's bright, familiar voice rose over the loop, riding the crowd noise, cutting through the commentary hum with the kind of energy that made even a siege sound like a party, and my rag stopped mid-stroke.

"And if you're just tuning in, I'm Yuna, and welcome to the last battle of Season Zero, live from Singapore, the biggest night of the beta!" Her voice pitched up slightly, matching the crowd roar underneath. "The Knights of the London Spire sit on 10,500 season points, Aogiri trails them by 250, and the Battle of the Snowy Plains is worth 300. That means Aogiri has to win this one to take the whole season, and the Knights only have to hold. The final window opened two hours ago, three days of seventy-two-hour death locks have worn this down to twenty Knights against twelve Aogiri, and every player who falls sits out three full days. And unlike a battle arena, this is the open world. Nobody here gets teleported anywhere, and whatever these players have actually earned back in Eryndal rides into this fight with them, so don't let the fancy gear on-screen fool you into thinking it's cosmetic."

Yuna. The name surfaced automatically, tied to Momo's whole personality lately, her voice blasting through a closed bedroom door most nights, singing along to tracks Yuna had performed in some virtual space in HGO. I didn't have the energy to be impressed tonight, but some habits didn't need energy to run, and my eyes tracked back to the screen before I'd decided to look.

The broadcast cut to a wide shot of a walled town sunk in the snow, a medieval city-state small enough to cross in ten minutes on foot, all gray stone and shuttered houses around a squat city hall with a banner snapping on its steps. Smoke hung low over the roofs. Along the bottom of the frame the overlay read **ZONE: CONTESTED**, and the WattsUp lightning bolt sat on the sponsor bar right there between two logos I didn't know, and I almost laughed. I squinted against the store lights glaring off the glass and dug the heel of my hand into one eye, like that would make me stop looking. It didn't.

Past the town the plains rolled out white toward the one pass that led back to Lunarest, and that was where the fight was really being won. Not in the open. The Knights of the London Spire had pulled back into the walls themselves, twenty of them tucked into shuttered houses and the shadow of the gatehouse, and they weren't holding a line at all. They were waiting.

Every Aogiri tag that came through the gate walked into a street the Knights had already built a kill zone for. The cam kept cutting to it. A lone Spellforger in midnight blue stepped off the road onto packed snow, and bolts and fire came out of three windows at once and folded him before his boots cleared the gatehouse. One more body out for seventy-two hours. The cam pulled back and I could see the whole shape of it, every window a firing slot, every alley a cut-off, the street a funnel with nowhere to run but forward.

That was the whole fight, and it was working. Aogiri's leader was stuck inside with eleven others, pinned in the city hall and a little house beside it with their banner on the hall steps, and he wasn't well. The overlay flagged it in small green letters beside his name, **POISONED**, a slow rot the Knights' casters had put on him that ate a little health every few seconds, and the only thing that cleared it was an antidote none of the twelve had left. The antidotes were on the people trying to reach him. Every one of them who died at the gate went on the seventy-two-hour lock, out of the fight for three days, and the ones who came back were throwing themselves at the same gate with the same kill zone waiting. Nobody had to charge in and finish the twelve. The Knights only had to keep the gate bleeding and let the poison do the work.

I hated how clean the read came. It was the tactics. It had always been the tactics, the reason I'd loved this game in the first place, who had the ground and who had the clock and where a fight actually broke, back when Eren and I spent whole nights drawing builds on graph paper and arguing over how a fight should be played. That was exactly why I kept my back to it now. It was still in there, and I hated that it was still in there. I dragged a hand back through my locs, a loose one slipping free again, and made myself breathe.

And they were playing like boneheads. The leader had one real choice and the poison was eating it. Either he cured himself and broke out with the eleven while a wave hit the gate from the other side at the same moment, so the Knights had to choose a front, or he sent a runner out through the caverns under the plains, a way around the gate nobody on the broadcast had even mentioned, to meet the wave and bring the antidote back. Anything, as long as it was a decision. The gate was London's single point of failure too, one input, one output, no second line, the kind of thing my father would have glanced at and asked where the redundancy was, and the Knights were thin there. Every minute the leader sat on the steps was a minute the poison took from his health bar, and the banner was bound to him. If he went, it went.

Aogiri's leader didn't move. He sat on the hall steps with his shield across his knees and watched his own health tick down, one tick, then another, calling out to no one, waiting for a wave that kept dying at the gate before it ever reached him. The cam lingered on his face, a big man with his jaw set like he meant to outlast the poison on stubbornness alone. My hands went flat on the counter.

"Pick something," I said out loud, low, to no one, already knowing how this ended, and I hated that I'd said it. I turned my back on the screen and went back to wiping the same spot I'd already cleaned twice tonight. "Break out or cure it. Do something."

Behind me, sound cracked out of the speaker, sharp electronic hits stacking fast over each other, a double-kill chime that made the crowd explode, and I winced as the volume spiked. Then the roar changed. Something had gone down on the hall steps.

"His health bar's gone! Aogiri's leader is down, and the banner goes with him!" Yuna's voice cut through the noise, half-shouting. "The zone belongs to the Knights alone, and the ten-minute clock is running!"

I didn't turn around for any of it. I wiped the same spot a third time and a fourth, listening to the crowd climb as the minutes bled off, a long dull groan rolling through it every time another Aogiri tag tried the gate and didn't make it. Nobody left to contest the zone. Then one low, resonant tone, the kind that meant something had just ended for good.

I already knew what it sounded like when a hold timer ran out.

"That's game!" Yuna's voice cut through the eruption. "London Spire takes Season Zero, forty million yen and the trophy, in front of a sold-out house here in Singapore and however many millions of you are watching from home!"

I let myself turn back around just in time to catch the confetti falling, gold and white streaming down over the stage. Tough, losing like that. I felt nothing about it.

Forty million. The thought went exactly where it always went. Even a tenth of that paid for the surgery outright. No more of Chiyo quietly covering gaps she thought I didn't notice. No more Tabuchi calls.

The broadcast didn't cut to credits. It cut to a soundstage draped in silver and blue, and the enormous cartoon bear ambled out from behind a curtain to real, delighted applause, both in the arena and, faintly, from the little speaker Gato kept behind the counter for his news when he worked the front.

Kazuku. Nobody knew his real face. Just the bear, round-bellied and grinning, HGO's compass-rose logo stitched across a vest that always looked freshly pressed no matter how many times he wore it on camera. He stood on a stage animated through holographic projection tech Singapore had been pioneering for years, his avatar as real to the crowd there as if he'd walked out in person.

"Congratulations to London Spire," the bear said, arms spread wide like he meant to hug the whole arena, his voice warm and genuine in a way that always made me suspicious. "And to every guild that fought for that stage tonight. You've reminded the world why we do this."

He paused, let the applause roll through and fade, then his expression shifted into something brighter.

"And now, since you've all been so patient with me."

Gold light bloomed across the screen behind him, a map pattern I recognized a half-second before my brain caught up. Not one continent anymore but five. Five separate landmasses where there had only ever been one, each one detailed enough that I could see mountain ranges and coastlines rendered in fine, deliberate strokes.

"Eryndal is growing," Kazuku said, and the arena noise swelled again, louder this time, people on their feet. "The Hearthfire expansion arrives January 1st. Five new continents, each one favoring the people born to it. Kingdoms and cities you'll build and defend yourselves, in a world where the people already living there notice exactly who you are and what you've done. Earn their trust, or don't. And before the real wars come, we're opening a new way to fight. Siege. Five guilds, one neutral zone, every guild starting with nothing but a camp and a flag. Build it, hold it, outlast the rest. And for the players who would rather fight for sport than for land, there's a league waiting for you when the season starts."

My weight shifted forward slightly, like I could lean into the screen and see the map more clearly if I just got close enough. Then I caught it and stepped back, and found the counter's edge with my hand again.

"And to christen it," the bear went on, "we'll be hosting the Crownfall Siege on Christmas Day, in a neutral zone nobody owns. Twenty of your guild on the field, open to every new character, with ten million yen on the table." The number bloomed in gold behind him, **¥10,000,000**, and the arena roared for it. He reached behind his back and produced an oversized access drive, the kind of theatrical prop that only worked because he was ten feet tall and made of CG, gold light spilling off it like it was the most important object in the world. "Open beta starts tonight, and Crownfall opens Season One. Everything you earn from the moment you log in counts toward the season. Points are yours alone, but Crownfall is a guild event, so bring your guild. And whether you're stepping into Eryndal for the first time, or you've been circling the idea of setting up shop in the new economy, bring your business here. There's never been a cheaper way in. For the next seventy-two hours only, every HGO access drive and every rig is fifty percent off. And about your existing avatar, every closed-beta character locks at its level once the expansion launches. Legacy Points waiting to be spent or cashed out on your terms, and once you're on a new avatar the old one is retired."

The number appeared on-screen beneath him, clean white text against the dark: **50% OFF - 72 HOURS ONLY**.

I read it twice, the same way I'd read Chiyo's text, making sure the number was real and not something my exhausted brain had invented to torment me.

Half off. For three days. I did the math automatically, the same way I did every math problem that involved money I didn't have. If I bought it right now, there was enough. Barely. And ten million yen on the table for one night, from the one game on earth I'd sworn I'd never touch.

I shook it off. Not tonight, and not this game. Tonight was a shift, and tomorrow was another one, and a sale was only a sale. I'd watched people burn their savings on less.

My thumb found the edge of the counter and pressed there, hard enough that my nail went white, a loose loc slipping across my face that I didn't bother pushing back. My mind kept running the number anyway, a stubborn little machine that never knew when to quit, and I made it stop.

It's not like I was making bank here. Gato was a good guy, but this job had a ceiling and I'd hit it six months ago. Chiyo was covering more than she should, Mom's bills weren't getting smaller, and the Tabuchis weren't going to stop calling just because we kept ignoring them. If I didn't do something, Chiyo was going to make the Tochi decision for me, and I'd be walking back into that school with my tail between my legs. I scrubbed a hand down my face, my palm dragging over eyes gone dry and gritty under the store lights.

I wasn't going to gamble the little I had on a game I couldn't even stand to hear. I picked the rag back up and told myself that was the end of it.

Red-gold headlights swept across the front window and my stomach dropped half a second before I understood why. A black car eased up to the curb outside the charge bays, slow and certain.

Through the windshield I could just make out a figure in dark clothes, hands steady on the wheel as the car powered down.

The driver's door swung open first, and blonde hair caught the streetlight before anything else registered. Black team jacket zipped to the throat, a silver lotus crossed with two staffs stitched into the chest, the emblem catching the light like it wanted to be noticed.

My posture shifted as soon as I recognized who it was.

Eren. I knew that emblem like I knew the scar over my own eye. I'd seen it enough times on streams and burned it into my memory whether I wanted it there or not. Kansai Black Lotus. Eren's guild. Funded by Tabuchi money, built on a concept I'd helped him theorize back when we'd still been friends.

I let nothing show on my face. I went still instead, hands flat on the counter, breathing steady and controlled the way I always did when something was about to go wrong and there was no clean way to stop it.

The passenger door opened a second later. Silver hair, warmup jacket slung over one shoulder, still half in a practice kit that looked expensive even from across the lot. My eyes tagged the build before my memory supplied the name.

Haruto. Kansai Black Lotus's best player, the guy they'd built the guild around, the beta's Breakout Player of the Season, the kind of player people actually respected instead of just hyping for content.

Great. Exactly what I needed tonight.

Eren said something to Haruto that didn't carry through the window, gestured toward the charge bays like he was making a point about something, and the two of them started across the lot toward the front door, boots scuffing on wet asphalt, their breath fogging in the cold.

The bell over the door announced them before either one spoke, the sound cutting through the hum of the broadcast still running on the screen above my head.

Eren's eyes found my face under the fluorescent store lights and stalled there, recognition landing a half-second late, then all at once, his expression shifting from neutral into something that wasn't quite a smile but was trying to be one.

"Well, well." He glanced sideways at Haruto like he needed a witness for this, his grin widening. "Guess who we've got here. Our dear friend Tatsu, working the night shift. Didn't know you'd taken up residence at the WattsUp."

My face didn't move. Some small muscle around my mouth flattened out, tightened slightly, the closest thing to a reaction I was willing to give away for free.

Dear friend. Right. That's what we're calling it now.

"Bay's open," I said, nodding toward the window and the black car parked beyond it, my voice level and professional, the same tone I used for every customer who walked through that door and didn't deserve anything extra. "Card or account."

Eren didn't answer that. He leaned into the counter instead, both hands flat on the surface I'd just cleaned, close enough that I caught his cologne cutting through the cold night air he'd brought in with him, something sharp and expensive.

"You watching the broadcast?" Eren asked, tilting his chin up toward the screen where Kazuku's bear was still grinning behind scrolling captions about the expansion launch. "Crazy night, right? We got bounced out of the top eight."

"Heard about that," I said, and left it there.

I didn't say what I was actually thinking, that a guild built on a stolen concept always hit a ceiling the concept alone couldn't push through, and that the only reason Eren's had gotten as far as it had was his dad's money buying him gear good enough to cover the gaps.

Behind Eren, Haruto had gone quiet, his eyes moving between the two of us like he was working out a math problem of his own.

"My father wanted me to remind you the account's still open," Eren said, his voice dropping into that lower register he always used when he wanted to sound like a man.

"I haven't forgotten," I said. I kept my hands exactly where they were, breathing even and controlled, my pulse steady in my throat. "Bay's open. Card or account."

Something in Eren's easy expression flickered, just for a second. It might have been irritation, or disappointment, or maybe just the faint recognition that this conversation wasn't going the way he'd pictured it going.

"Card," Eren said, pulling it from his wallet and tapping it against the reader without looking away from my face, holding eye contact like it was a challenge I was supposed to rise to. "I hope your mother's doing better. I heard she's still at the hospital. That's rough, man. Really rough."

Fake as always.

My grip on the counter's edge tightened at the knuckles for exactly one breath, my jaw setting hard enough that I felt it in my teeth, then loosened again before the tension could spread anywhere visible. Nothing on my face moved. I watched the charge indicator climb on the screen in front of me, green light by green light, tick by tick, until the transaction cleared and Eren finally stepped back from the counter.

Eren turned toward the door without another word, his jacket catching the overhead light as he moved, the Kansai Black Lotus emblem glinting silver across his back.

Haruto lingered a beat longer than he needed to, his weight still resting slightly against the counter.

"Guess I'll see you in Eryndal, then," Haruto said, quiet enough that the words didn't carry past the counter, just between the two of us. He nodded once toward the TV, small and deliberate. "You're too sharp to be working a register. Hope you know that."

I gave him a nod back, nothing more spent on it than that, my expression still flat, still unreadable.

Haruto straightened up, pulled his jacket tighter against the cold, and followed Eren out into the night.

The door swung shut behind them with a soft chime, and quiet folded back over the store like it had never left.

I stood there a moment longer than I needed to, watching through the window as the black car's taillights disappeared down the block, red glow fading into the ambient neon of Shibuya at midnight. The hum of the charging cables outside was the only sound left in the place.

He just carries the message. He doesn't even know what's in the envelope. The thought came flat and certain, the kind I'd stopped questioning somewhere between the funeral and the third phone call. Whatever Eren's father was really after, it had never been Eren's to know, and I ran my thumb once along the edge of the counter before I reached for the lights.

I looked up at the screen. The bear was gone. In its place, the discount banner ran on a loop, gold numbers ticking down a countdown that had just crossed under seventy-two hours.

**71:42:18**

**71:42:17**

I took one final breath and went to close up.

I moved through closing on autopilot, muscle memory carrying me when my brain didn't want to be present for any of it. I killed the bays one at a time, the digital readouts blinking dark in sequence. Counted the drawer twice, same total both times, bagged it for Gato's morning deposit. Wiped down the counter one last pass, out of habit more than need, the rag coming up clean. Hit the lights, stepped out into the cold, the door locking itself behind me with a heavy mechanical clunk that echoed off the empty lot.

Around back, the Haya EB moped was sitting exactly where I'd left it, old paint gone matte in patches under the yellow security light, the helmet hanging off the handlebar where I always left it when I knew nobody else would be back here. I pulled it on, tucked the loose loc back into place before it could get caught in the strap, and swung a leg over the seat, the cold leather biting through my jeans.

The touchscreen between the handlebars flickered awake under my thumb the way it always did, half a second of hesitation like it didn't trust me yet, the old hardware taking its time. I waited it out, thumb still pressed flat to the glass, until the dash finally lit green and the charge indicator climbed and stalled at two bars, not the full it should have read after a day on the charger.

I kicked the stand up, rolled it forward off the curb with my feet, and pulled out onto wet asphalt, letting Shibuya blur past in long smears of orange and white, neon bleeding together in the rain that had picked up again without me noticing. The streets were emptier now, past the point where the last trains had run and the people still out were the ones who didn't have anywhere better to be. I opened it up down an empty stretch, the hum of the electric motor climbing under me, rain needling cold against my cheeks where the helmet didn't quite cover. The hum stuttered once, a dip in the climb like the motor had lost its grip on something, then caught again.

Some track was playing low through the scooter's tiny built-in speaker, one of Yuna's, something Momo had dropped into my queue two weeks ago without asking and I'd never bothered deleting.

The ride home ran out from under me before I was ready for it. Two blocks short, the Haya EB slowed under my hand, the dash blinking dark for a full second and coming back sulky and dim, and then the motor quit altogether, the hum dropping out of the frame like someone had pulled a plug. I thumbed the throttle twice. Nothing. I swung off into the rain and walked it the rest of the way, boots splashing through the gutter, rain needling my neck, my jaw tight as the familiar six-story building came up on my left, the one I'd been looking at every night for two years without ever really seeing it anymore. A striped green awning caught the streetlight overhead, faded a shade lighter than I remembered it being when we'd first moved in, and the plain glass doors of the lobby threw back the dead headlight of the scooter I was pushing before I steered it around the corner.

I parked around back on reflex, in the spot that wasn't technically mine but nobody else ever used, and came in through the side door the hotel staff used during the day.

She was still at the front desk. Alone.

I stopped in the doorway to the lobby longer than I meant to, just taking it in before she noticed me. Chiyo behind the counter in her blazer, the one she wore when she was working a shift instead of just managing, red hair falling loose over one shoulder in a way that meant she'd been standing there too long without a break. She was checking something on the reservation screen, eyes focused with the kind of intensity that meant she'd been doing this for hours and wasn't done yet.

A woman who led Japan's national volleyball team onto an Olympic court, still working a night shift that should have had two other people on it. I didn't have to ask why they weren't here.

She looked up and the tired dropped off her face on purpose, replaced with something warmer.

"You saw my message?" she asked, not looking away from me, her hands still moving across the keyboard. "About Tochi."

"Yeah," I said, my own hands going into my jacket pockets, shoulders loose, posture casual in a way that took effort to maintain.

"I need an answer soon, Tatsu." She said it plainly, no edge in her voice. "If you don't decide, I'm going to have to make the decision for you. You know that, right?"

I didn't answer that. I didn't have to. She'd meant it as a promise, not a threat, and I understood it exactly that way. She loved me too much to let me sit in this limbo forever, stuck between a job that wasn't going anywhere and a school I'd walked away from for reasons she'd never pushed me to explain.

I gave her a nod that didn't commit to anything, the same nod I'd been giving her for months whenever this conversation came up, and headed for the stairs, feeling her eyes shift back to the screen.

She was going to decide for me whether I wanted her to or not. WattsUp wasn't cutting it, and we both knew it.

I took the steps two at a time, my boots landing heavy on each one, the sound echoing in the stairwell.

So either I went back to Tochi with my tail tucked between my legs, or I did something first. Something she couldn't argue with. Something that proved I wasn't just wasting time.

Upstairs, the apartment was mostly dark. Momo's door was cracked open, a wedge of pink light spilling into the hallway, her voice humming along under her headphones to something with way too much reverb, a melody I recognized from the ride home. I caught the edge of her phone screen through the gap, @Yuna_N9 pulled up in her browser, some livestream replay looping in the corner, Momo's face lit by the glow as she mouthed the words without actually singing them out loud.

In the front room, Taji was still awake on the couch, wrapped in a blanket he'd clearly stolen from someone's bed, probably Momo's, based on the color, watching the exact same broadcast I'd just left behind at WattsUp. The London Spire trophy shot was paused on the screen, confetti frozen mid-fall, London Spire's captains in gold and white holding hardware that probably weighed more than it looked like it did.

"They actually did it," Taji said, not looking up, his tone pitched somewhere between impressed and envious, assuming the way he always did that I had no idea what he was talking about and needed the context explained.

"Yeah," I said, dropping onto the couch beside him and stealing a corner of the blanket back without asking. "It was on at work. Aogiri threw it at the end. Their leader sat on his hands and let the poison take him."

Taji turned to look at me, eyebrows raised, like I'd said something that didn't match the script he'd been expecting. "You actually watched it? Like, the whole fight?"

"Most of it," I said, leaning back into the couch, one arm stretched along the back cushions. "Corporate's got it on every screen in the building. It's not like I was busy tonight."

Taji cracked a small smile at that despite himself, the kind he tried not to give up too easily these days now that he was eleven and thought acting unimpressed made him look older. He shoved the rest of the blanket over without being asked, a peace offering that didn't need words attached to it.

I sat there in my own living room and let it land, the broadcast still paused on-screen, Momo's muffled humming bleeding through the walls, Taji's blanket warm against my legs, Chiyo two floors down covering a shift that a retired national-team legend shouldn't have to work at all. None of them had asked this.

The Haya EB's dash was still sulking at the back of my mind, that long blank second before it had come back dim. I pushed myself up off the couch.

"Back in a minute," I said. Taji gave me a nod without looking away from the frozen trophy shot.

My room was the last door down the hall, the converted corner of the apartment my father had worked out of for most of my life, and I didn't bother with the overhead. The desk lamp was enough. The room smelled faintly like old solder and cold circuit boards, the way it had since long before the funeral, and two pale grooves still ran along the floorboards from the desk to the door where a wheelchair had worn them down.

Dad's tech manual sat on the shelf above the desk, spine cracked from years of use, pages dog-eared in a dozen places. The EB had to be fixable. Everything he'd built could be fixed. I pulled the manual down and flipped to the section on the controller board, both hands flat on the desk, my thumb running down the margin notes in his slanted handwriting, looking for the line about the dash resetting itself under load.

Something slid out from between the pages and landed face-up on the desk. A single sheet, folded in thirds, soft at the creases like it had been sealed in there a long time. His handwriting again, but not the margin shorthand. Slower. Pressed harder into the paper.

**[Tatsuya,]**

**[If you're reading this, then I'm gone, and I didn't manage to deal with the Tabuchis. I can't explain everything, not on paper. But what's in this manual is what they need, so don't give it to them. Not a page, not a drawing. They've been pushing me for a long time to sell the business out from under us, and I haven't, and I hope that by the time you read this they still haven't gotten hold of anything that matters to their plans.]**

**[I'm sorry. I know I'm leaving this on you and Chiyo, and that isn't fair. But I know you can carry it.]**

**[If you want the truth, talk to the bear. He's the only one who has it, and he'll tell you. Just be careful who is listening.]**

**[A man who couldn't walk once built a place where he could run. The world there doesn't know my name. Maybe it's time it did.]**

I read it once for the words and once for the date I couldn't find, because there wasn't one. My hands had gone flat on the desk again without me telling them to. At the bottom, where his name should have been, there was only a print in dark ink, smudged at one edge. I tilted the page toward the lamp and squinted until the shape resolved: a paw, four toes and a wide pad. A bear's.

The bear. Talk to the bear. I turned the words over and they wouldn't sit still. Then another line surfaced, one I'd heard ten thousand times over the last year and tried not to hear once. Bring your business here. I stopped breathing. He couldn't mean. He couldn't mean the man on the stage, the one who'd just handed a stadium an oversized drive, the one who had a ten-foot cartoon bear for a face.

But the first-gen pod. The one Dad had spent his last years on, in this room, in a chair that wasn't built for the work. It had never been a side project. It had been a piece of this. My breath went out through my nose, slow and controlled, and a loose loc slid across my face that I didn't push back. I'd spent a year telling myself the pieces only looked wrong because I was grieving. Standing there with the page still warm from my hands, I knew I was the only one in that apartment who'd been pretending.

Everything lined up the way a bad sum finally lines up. The calls that kept circling back to his paperwork. The Tabuchis who wanted what was in this book more than they wanted their money. Dad hadn't wanted them to have it. He had known exactly what they'd do when he was gone, and he'd left the answer in the only place a man like me would look, inside a manual I'd only opened because of a scooter, and with the only person who could prove any of it. A bear who answered, if the letter was right, inside the game.

That was the part I kept coming back to. I couldn't walk into HGO with nothing and expect the man behind it to take my call. A goblin with a headline wouldn't do it either. But a player carrying something only my father could have built, a gadget, a design, a piece of his logic that nobody else in the game had seen, and that player standing at the front of a guild when ten million yen and every eye in the world was on Crownfall? That might get his attention. And it paid. Whatever else the game was, it paid, and Mom's bills hadn't stopped coming just because I'd found a letter.

I refolded the page along its old creases, slid it back in at the controller diagrams, and set the manual on the shelf exactly where it had been, spine out, because Chiyo and the twins weren't supposed to know there was anything in it to find. I killed the lamp and went back down the hall.

Taji hadn't moved. I dropped back onto the couch beside him, the blanket warm against my legs again.

I opened the app with my bank balance in it, the one that told me exactly how much room I had to maneuver and how much I didn't.

Rent covered. Mom's hospital bill covered, barely, with Chiyo carrying most of the actual weight, groceries for one more week if we were tight about it. And underneath all of that, if I moved right now, there was enough left over for a drive.

My thumb swiped to the Nexus Enterprises purchase screen, the ad blinking next to a number that counted down the remaining time of the sale.

If I did this, I couldn't forget why I was doing it. Not for me. Not to prove something to Eren or show off to Haruto or get Loop clout or any of that. It was for them. It was for a man who couldn't walk and the name nobody in that world knew. My eyes flicked toward Taji, who was still staring at the frozen trophy shot like he was trying to memorize every detail.

Fine. I pulled in a breath through my nose, held it longer than I needed to, and let it out slow and controlled. Doing it.

I pressed my thumb down on the purchase button before I could talk myself out of it, before the rational part of my brain could start listing all the reasons this was a bad idea and I should wait and save more.

A small green confirmation bloomed on the screen a moment later, text appearing in clean white letters: **Purchase Complete. Your HGO Access Drive will be available for pickup within 24 hours.**

I locked my phone and set it face-down on the couch beside me, my hand coming up to rub the back of my neck. I couldn't waste a single day. I had to figure things out early. Every hour I spent in Eryndal from here on had to count for something real.

No noob bullshit. Find the fastest route to turn this into money she couldn't argue with, and a way to put my father's name in front of the bear.

I didn't remember closing my eyes.
