# Chapter Ten: Assembling the Assault Team

Five dead on the east patrol. That couldn't have been a normal party, and the thought wouldn't leave me alone.

Sevish didn't slow. He took the last ten paces to the war tent in long strides with Wyx half a step behind him and Kesh stumbling after them, and I walked with my eyes on nothing until Ru stopped short and I nearly ran into his back.

"My bad, Ru," I said.

"A-are you okay, Nykri?" His big hand had come out to steady me, and a stutter still clung to the front of his words.

"Yeah, I'm fine. You?"

"Just a l-little nervous about the meeting," Ru said. "There is a lot at stake. We have to get out of here alive."

"Yeah," I said.

*"For them, life and death means a lot more than it does for me."*

Past him the camp stood quiet. The stew pot sat cold beside the stone-ringed fire pit, the hay cart stood where it always had beside the big tent, the two blue banners hung limp on their poles, and the hide tents along the slope were tied shut with nobody between them. Sharpened stakes ringed the camp on the hillside now, set tight, and what was left of the clan stood posted along them under torches, with gaps in the line. The sky over the cliff was clear and full of stars, and the waterfall hissed behind the tents.

Two things stuck as I walked. The first was the ways in and out. The track left through the stakes at the front, and the only other way down was the cliffside along the wall, gullies and ledges a goblin might climb if he had to, longer and harder than the path and no use with armor or a pack. Behind the camp there was only sheer rock and falling water, so nothing could come at us from the back. The second was the gear. Every goblin on the line carried the same club and the same plain round shield of hide over wood, like one issue out of one store. I didn't know where they'd gotten it.

Bone totems capped the poles either side of the flap. It fell shut behind us on canvas heat and old leather, and the noise of the camp went flat and far away.

The tent was round, with timber ribs curving up to the center pole and the lantern hanging from it. The back wall faced the cliff, and through it I could feel the cold coming off the rock.

Sevish went straight through to the far side of the table and lowered himself into the heavy chair there, the chest and barrels stacked tidy against the back wall behind him, his axe propped against the chair arm at his right hand. Wyx took her place at his left. I took the near side at the center, the flap at my back. Kesh dropped onto a barrel along the left wall and rubbed his knee. Ru stopped inside the flap on the right, one shoulder to the canvas, where a gap in the cloth gave him a strip of the camp to watch. He stood with his head bent under the cross rope above the entrance, still not used to his own height.

For a moment nobody spoke while everyone settled. A chair creaked, the lantern steadied on its hook, and Kesh's breathing slowed.

"Kesh," Sevish said. "How many on that road?"

"Four," Kesh said between breaths. "Maybe five. Kesh could not get close."

Sevish grunted and turned his head. "Wyx, could you do me a favor? Go outside and get the men into a defensive formation along the palisade walls. Anyone who comes within sight of the camp, I want to know. Hold the perimeter and let nothing past the stakes."

Before Wyx could answer, the flap lifted behind me. Her eyes flicked past my shoulder. "Of course, brother," she said. "But could I hear the scouting report first?"

I glanced back. A lean goblin girl had slipped in, dust on her knees, a wrap of dark cloth over one shoulder, her pale green eyes steady under a narrow brow. A **[ZINKA: LV 7 - HOBGOBLIN]** nameplate resolved over her head, white.

"It was four, chief," she said, and gave Kesh a flat look. "The fifth was his own shadow."

Sevish didn't laugh. A breath of laughter escaped me before I could stop it, and I covered my mouth with my fist. Kesh scratched the back of his head and studied the dirt floor.

"Ru can go and assist," I said, and looked at the big hobgoblin standing under the cross rope with his hands loose, waiting. "With so many of us down and so many in here talking, there's hardly anyone on the line. If they pushed now, we'd be finished. The more hands out there, the better."

Sevish studied me for a moment, heavy and patient, a look that asked a question without saying it. Then he gave me a slow nod. Wyx's brows rose, and for a second she looked at me instead of the scout.

I turned to Ru. "Get the line in order. Raise the inner barricade behind the stakes, pull every goblin back inside, and make sure each one is armed and listening. If anyone won't listen, use a little force."

Ru straightened until the cross rope brushed his hair. "Understood," he said, and ducked out through the flap.

A small marker with his name appeared on the mini-map in the corner of my vision and slid along the edge of camp, and the gray around it cleared as it went. *"Huh. So that's how that works."*

"That is not the first step you have taken tonight, Nykri," Wyx said quietly.

"Now go join him, Kesh," Sevish said. "He will need someone who knows the lanes."

"Kesh go," Kesh said, already off his barrel, and ducked out after Ru.

Zinka stepped up to the near side of the table, on my right. "Four of them in a red tent on the shoulder of the road," she said, and set one finger on the map. "Do not go near that tent. Something is wrong around it. When we crept close to see who they were, before they ever struck, we grew weaker and slower with every step. That is how the patrol was taken, and the two scouts after them. Kesh and I came from different routes, and that is the only reason we got away."

"That explains how they've been taking us out one at a time," I said. "And the tent isn't randomly placed either. Whoever they are, they knew exactly what they were doing."

Wyx's head turned, her amber eyes narrowing.

It felt oddly like the situation Aogiri found itself in at Thornwatch. I wasn't going to let that happen to us.

"Thank you, Zinka," Sevish said.

"Go and join Ru and Kesh," Wyx said. "Follow his instructions."

"Yes, Wyx," Zinka said, and went out through the flap.

When I looked up, Sevish had lifted his axe from the chair arm. He rolled his shoulders once, slow, and the lantern flame leaned away from him. He was done sitting.

"Enough talk," he said. "I take this axe down that road and the best I have behind me, and Wyx beside me. We have the numbers. We end it before the torches burn out."

"You'd win," I said. "I don't doubt it. But we don't know how strong those adventurers are. They have a ward and a caster, and they could be weak or far stronger than they look. We can't tell, because nobody got close enough to scout without being struck and having to run. We know almost nothing. If we go down there with the few we have and they're stronger than us, what if we don't come back up? That's the risk, going in without a plan that doesn't put every life we have in jeopardy."

"There is also the camp," Wyx said. She rested one hand on the map beside the stakes. "Brother and I are the walls. Anyone who passes the outer stakes meets me. If I fail, they meet you. If the two of us go down that road, the stakes hold nothing."

"I take my counsel from my sister, and from Shiv," Sevish said, and the anger came up under his voice, slow and heavy. "You are neither."

"Then take it from Shiv." I put my palms on the table. "Last I checked, he put Ru under my calling. Ru is also a member of Thrash, the same one two of your enforcers nearly pummeled. I'm not trying to split the clan or take it from you. But this is what Shiv was talking about, the fighting among ourselves. There has to be a way to handle this without putting everyone's lives in jeopardy. You might win that fight, Sevish. Can the ones you just sent to hold those walls say the same?"

Sevish didn't answer. His hand had stopped on the haft, and for a long moment the only sound in the tent was the lantern ticking against the pole. Wyx crossed her arms, and the corner of her mouth moved, though she said nothing. A notification flashed in the corner of my eye and I let it.

"Fine," Sevish said, flat and slow. "Then what are you suggesting? We sit here and let these bastards bleed us out?"

"No." A short laugh got out of me before I could stop it. "I'm not suggesting we sit here with our hands folded, and I'm not suggesting we be walking targets either. Give me enough time to organize a proper plan. I'll put together a small team and hit them from two sides while they're recovering. I'll make it work if you give me the time."

"Shiv put him under your calling," Sevish said at last, slowly. "Then I will not disobey Shiv." His hand tightened on the haft. "Handle the ones at the bottom of the hill. You have until the east torches burn out. If they are not handled, I handle it my way."

"Fair enough," I said. "And if I handle it, I hope we can work together a lot smoother after."

"Hah." The rough laugh came out low and short. His eyes stayed on mine, two alphas in one small room, neither of us looking away. I felt my own temper rising to meet his, and for one second I forgot that he could end me with a single swing.

"We have to get off," Leon said in my ear. "The VRSA inspector's here."

The anger went out of me all at once. I let out a breath, and my hand loosened on the edge of the table. I had been about to shout at someone who could end me with one swing.

"Enough," I said. "I'm heading back to my tent to prepare."

I turned my back on both of them and shoved the flap aside.

The holding tent stood where I'd left it, the bedroll still flattened where I'd slept, the oil lamp low on its hook. I sat down on the bedroll.

"Sit," Leon said. "Hold still."

The lamp smeared into one long white line, and then the world went out.

The lid hissed open on the ceiling of the pod room, soft lantern light after the dark of the visor. I sat up slow, and my joints complained the way they always did after a long dive. The inspector was already at the monitor bay, crouched in front of the open panel with his black suit pressed flat against the chair, his long black hair tied low at the neck, a bang falling across one lens of his glasses. His tools lay in a neat row on a cloth beside him.

Leon stood next to him, one hip against the desk, glasses slipping down his nose. He looked up when I moved.

"It hit the thermal limit," he said. "The override held. It's the stream that cooked it."

"Streaming roughly doubles the draw," the inspector said, flat, without looking up. "The unit was not built for that."

"Sorry about the pod," I said.

"Don't be." Leon pushed his glasses back up. "I've run this place a long time. I've watched a lot of streams on that screen." He nodded at the monitor in front of the inspector. "I never saw anything like what you just pulled."

"You could be my coach," I said, half a joke.

Leon didn't answer right away, still bent over the panel. Then, quieter, mostly to himself, "Coach. Yeah." A pause, like he was turning it over. "Yeah, I could see that."

"Give me the ten minutes," he said, louder. "Go stretch your legs. You've been under a long time."

I ducked into the bathroom at the end of the upstairs hall and braced my phone against the sink. The Romans I already knew: the legion's shields locked edge to edge, short swords working between them. For the rest I had a few minutes. The armies of China's Warring States had drilled their spear and shield lines until the front rank could take a charge, Japan's Sengoku armies had done the same with spear walls behind their shield screens, and the Vikings stood shoulder to shoulder with their shields overlapping like a fence. Different centuries, same idea: a line that holds, and something sharp behind it. I read until I had the shape of it, pocketed the phone, and went downstairs.

I took the spiral staircase down to the lounge. It was quiet for the hour, the clock over the counter reading 8:54, one desktop glowing at the far wall. The only person in the room was behind the front counter, and she looked up before I reached the last step.

She had white hair in a messy bun, ink running down both forearms to the wrist, a plain black tee and sharp gray eyes. A half-smirk.

"Hey, Nykri."

She'd used the handle instead of my name. I stopped with one hand on the rail.

"Relax," Arya said, folding her arms on the counter. "Leon said you were on the quest. I watched the stream on the screen out here, and your camp's in trouble." She pushed off the counter. "So I'll skip the speech. I want in. Your quest, your camp, your fight. Whatever's coming for that camp, I've seen where the guild chat thinks it goes next, and I'd rather be on the inside when it does."

"No."

"I haven't said what I'm offering."

"Still no." I kept my voice even. "I don't know you. I'm a goblin in a hole and I'm not putting anybody else in it."

"Easy." The smirk went a little crooked. "I've heard goblins bite. I'm friendly, I promise."

"I've been told that," I said.

Something moved behind her eyes, and she looked at the counter instead of me. "My crew fell apart early in the season. The point is I've got a build and nowhere to run it, and you've got a quest the whole server wants."

"What's your build?" I said finally.

"Elf, Spellforger, level ten." She shrugged one shoulder. "Mostly field spells. Static Lash, Tempest Fan, Storm Shear, Stormwall, and a fog spell called Hushveil that I never use."

"What does the fog do?"

"Hides whoever's standing in it. It's about as wide as a small room, it lasts fifteen seconds, and then it needs thirty to cool down." She counted it off on her fingers. "It eats mana fast and it does zero damage. Nobody builds around it."

"Does the tree let you push it further?"

Her eyes went briefly unfocused as she pulled her own screen open. "There's a branch past it. Silence. Anyone caught in the mist can't cast and can't call out to their party." Her mouth twisted. "Five points deep, and I've never looked at it twice."

The one with the staff cast the most, Zinka had said. A fog that shut a caster's mouth was the piece I was missing. The plan I'd been carrying finally had a middle, and for the first time since the alert my hands felt steady.

"Put the points there," I said. "Before you log in."

She stared at me for a long moment. "You want me to put everything I have into the one spell nobody takes, on the word of a goblin."

"Yes."

"I could lose all of it."

"You could." I didn't look away. "How many points are you sitting on?"

"Too many," she said, and laughed once, short and a little ragged. "Fine. I'm gambling it all on the weakest race in the game."

Half an hour, all in. Leon's the only one with the voice channel, so once I'm in your party we talk in person. Don't wait for me to call you."
