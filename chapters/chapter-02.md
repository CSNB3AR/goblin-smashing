# Chapter Two: Into the Game

I came awake wrong, disoriented and sluggish, and knew it immediately before I even opened my eyes. Sunlight instead of the dim pre-dawn gray I should have been waking up to, warm against my eyelids in a way that said it was way past morning. My body didn't do sunlight like this, not after a night shift, not without putting up a fight first.

My phone was still in my hand, the screen dark and cool against my palm. I thumbed it awake and the time hit me like cold water: **3:04 PM**.

What the hell. I was up off the couch before the thought finished, the blanket sliding to the floor in a heap, the television still running the same paused replay of London Spire lifting the trophy that it had been showing when I closed my eyes. Momo's door stood open now, her room empty, bed made in the haphazard way that meant she'd done it fast before leaving. Taji's side of the couch was cold, no sign of him anywhere in the apartment.

Nobody home but me, and the whole day already gone without my permission, half of my seventy-two-hour window burned while I'd been unconscious on a couch.

Slept through the whole damn morning. Great start.

A note sat on the kitchen counter, folded once, my name written across the front in Chiyo's handwriting. I picked it up and read it standing there in the middle of an empty apartment.

**[Left early with the twins for the Kyoto trip, didn't want to wake you after the night you had. We'll be back in six days. Please eat something real, not just convenience store rice balls. [Chiyo]**

Right. The trip. I'd known about it for two weeks, had it marked on the calendar stuck to the fridge, and had completely lost track of it.

My phone buzzed in my hand, the vibration startling me more than it should have.

Then again.

A text from an unknown number sat at the top of my notifications, sender marked as **Nexus Enterprises**.

**[Your HGO Access Drive order has been processed and is ready for pickup. Please collect within 72 hours of this notification. Pickup Code: 4472-KAZE]**

Whatever came next, it started today. And I was already behind.

I stood in the middle of the apartment, phone still warm in my hand, the Nexus Enterprises text glowing against my thumb. The pickup notice sat there like a dare I'd already accepted without meaning to.

No going back now. I let the screen go dark and dropped the phone on the couch.

The television was still paused on London Spire's trophy shot, confetti frozen mid-fall behind them like the world had stopped right at their peak. I grabbed the remote and killed it. Corporate could run HGO on every screen at the store, but I wasn't going to have it running in my living room.

I took my laptop off the kitchen counter and dropped onto the couch, the cushions sinking under me in the spot that was basically mine now. I opened the HGO Companion App, and I wasn't there for the game. I was there for the market. Prices loaded first, item sets sorted by zone and rarity tier. Blue gear running five figures in yen for a single piece. Legendary gear didn't even have listed prices, just auction-house estimates that made my stomach drop when I scrolled past them.

The sale had pulled in a flood of veterans cashing their avatars in for Legacy Points ahead of the lock, and the numbers on that page told me the same thing the ad had. There was real money moving through this game, and a new character with nothing could start sitting at the edge of it. I scrolled past all of it, looking for the baseline, the floor a newcomer could actually stand on without dropping money. Common gear cheap, uncommon attainable if you knew where to farm.

The race-select table was on the same page, six numbers running down each row. Courage, Power, Wisdom, Speed, Fortune, Energy. I'd seen versions of that table before, in forums and in the margins of graph paper, back when Eren and I used to sit across from each other at Tochi arguing over which build would break the game. I closed the tab before I got to the Elf row. That was Eren's build now, and I wasn't touching it. Human, probably. Humans split the difference on everything, the safe pick for someone who didn't care enough to want anything specific and didn't want to get locked into the wrong race.

I closed the laptop and set it on the coffee table, leaning back against the couch with both hands behind my head. I wasn't trying to be good at this game. I was trying to get paid by it, and I'd read enough. Nothing on a page was going to tell me how that worked. I had to go in and find out.

I grabbed my phone off the couch, the screen lighting up with the time and the bank balance I kept as a widget at the top of the screen.

**[¥47,320]**

I ignored it and unlocked the screen and opened Loop instead. My profile loaded, follower count sitting in the corner like it always did, a number that hadn't moved in six months.

**[9 followers]**

Nine people I didn't know, following an account I barely posted from. I closed it and pulled up a cafe booking app, filtering by ward, then by availability. Third-gen cafes across Shibuya came up first, sleek storefronts with waiting lists that stretched into next year, every single slot booked solid. I scrolled past them, already knowing what I'd find, the seventy-two-hour sale driving demand past anything the infrastructure could handle.

Second-gen pods weren't much better. The Tabuchi-backed cafes had availability, technically, but the earliest slot was March, three months out. Not waiting three months.

I kept scrolling, deeper into results I'd already written off as dead ends, when a single listing caught my eye halfway down the third page.

Sakura Arcade, a name I didn't recognize, marked with a small gray tag that read [Legacy Hardware - First-Gen Pods Available]. The description mentioned limited compatibility with newer titles, walk-ins accepted, and a single line at the bottom that made me stop scrolling entirely.

**[Free after 5 PM on weekdays.]**

Free? I clicked through to the full listing. One pod, first-generation Kazuhaya Tech, the same hardware my father built before Nexus Enterprises bought the patent rights.

It shouldn't run at all. Not unless you knew exactly what was underneath the casing. And I did, or close enough. Dad's notes went back further than its early development. Which meant it was possible. That was all I had to go on.

I checked the time. 3:47 PM.

Sakura Arcade's free window opened in just over an hour, and I still had to pick up the drive from Nexus first. I stood, grabbed my jacket off the back of the couch where I'd left it, and headed for my room, Dad's old work room. I pushed the door open to a space that still smelled faintly like old solder and circuit boards, a smell that never quite left no matter how many times Momo complained about it.

Dad's tech manual sat on the shelf above my desk, spine cracked from years of use, pages dog-eared in a dozen places where he'd marked something worth coming back to. The folded note was still tucked in at the controller diagrams, right where I'd left it. I pulled the manual down, the weight of it familiar in my hands, and tucked it under my arm without thinking twice.

Might need this. Just a hunch.

Then I was out the door and down the stairs to the Haya EB, parked where I'd left it. I pulled the helmet over my head, tucked my locs in so I could bring down the visor, and pressed my hand to the touchscreen to start it up. I kicked the stand up and pushed out toward the asphalt, the directions guiding me toward Nexus.

• • •

Nexus Enterprises' flagship store took up half a block in Ginza, glass and brushed steel rising three stories over a plaza with more foot traffic than I'd seen anywhere outside a WattsUp on a holiday weekend. I'd been down here maybe twice in my life, both times as a kid. The place didn't look like it sold anything so much as it looked like a museum for tech people wished they could afford.

I pushed through the front doors and the smell hit me first, something clean and faintly metallic, antiseptic almost.

The ground floor read more showroom than store, third-gen helmets mounted on individual pedestals under warm spotlights, HGO's compass rose etched into the glass casing around each one, reflections bouncing off polished floors that looked like nobody was allowed to actually walk on them. A wall-sized display looped highlights from last night's battle on a screen taller than I was, Yuna's voice cutting through the ambient noise every time the reel restarted.

"...and that's game, London Spire takes Season Zero, three hundred points and the Snowy Plains..."

Of course they were playing that on repeat. I kept my head down and kept moving.

The pickup counter sat toward the back, past a bank of demo pods where a handful of kids waited their turn to try on a helmet for five free minutes. A line had formed anyway, everyone holding the same Nexus Enterprises confirmation text I had on my own phone.

I joined the line and kept my hands in my pockets, watching the guy ahead of me collect a small package, the sleeve glossy with the Nexus logo, the compass rose stamped in gold across the front. The same package got handed over three more times before I reached the counter myself.

"Confirmation number," the attendant said, not looking up from his tablet.

I rattled it off from memory, 4472-KAZE, and watched the guy scan something on his screen before disappearing into the back. He came out a moment later with the same package he'd handed everyone else.

"Drive's pre-loaded with your license," the attendant said, still not making eye contact. "You're good to go."

"Appreciate it," I said, taking the package and putting it in my backpack, and turning back toward the door before the guy could launch into an upsell pitch about warranty extensions or premium helmets I couldn't afford and didn't need.

Outside, the sun was already dropping behind the buildings, late-afternoon light going orange at the edges, the kind of light that said you had maybe an hour before everything went dark. I checked my phone. 4:22 PM. Sakura Arcade's free window opened in thirty-eight minutes, and I still had to get all the way back across town to Shibuya, navigate whatever traffic was clogging up the main routes, and hope the place actually existed and wasn't just some abandoned storefront with a listing nobody had bothered to take down.

I pulled my helmet on, the visor clicking into place with the sound I'd heard every morning for two years, and kicked the Haya EB to life. The engine caught on the first try, humming up through my palms the way it always did when it was actually behaving, and I opened it up down the nearest stretch of clear road, downtown thinning out into the streets I actually knew, the ones that didn't make me feel like I was trespassing just by being there.

• • •

Sakura Arcade sat on a side street I didn't recognize, tucked between a shuttered ramen shop with faded kanji on the sign and a convenience store that looked like it was barely hanging on. The building itself was older than most of the surrounding blocks, brick and wood paneling where everything else had gone glass and steel, warm pendant lights glowing through the front windows in a way that read more like a lounge than a gaming cafe.

This is it? Doesn't look like much. I parked the Haya EB around the side, out of the way, pulled my helmet off and hung it on one handlebar, then went to the door with my backpack on.

I stepped through the front door into a space that smelled faintly like old carpet and wild scents. Themed gaming chairs lined the walls in neat rows, each one facing a monitor, most of them dark and unused.

Behind the counter, a guy in his mid-thirties looked up from a tablet, dark hair pulled back in a small knot, sleeves rolled to his elbows.

"Can I help you?" he asked, setting the tablet down and straightening up slightly.

"I'm here for a pod," I said, stepping up to the counter. "First-gen, if you've got availability."

The guy's expression shifted, not quite a wince, more like someone who'd had this exact conversation too many times today and was already tired of repeating himself. "I do, but I'm gonna warn you up front that they're first-gen Kazuhaya Tech. They don't play well with newer titles. I've had to turn away a dozen HGO hopefuls since the sale hit. It's not gonna run the way you think it will."

"I know," I said, keeping my voice level, not defensive, just matter-of-fact. "That's fine."

The guy studied me for a second, like he was trying to figure out whether I actually knew what I was walking into or was just saying I did to sound confident, then shrugged and pulled a clipboard off the counter, flipping it around and sliding it across with a pen. "Sign in. Name, time in. You've got the back room to yourself, only one pod's actually functional, the other one's just a shell at this point."

I took the pen and wrote my name across the top line in clean block letters, the same way I'd been signing forms since middle school. Tatsuya Kazehaya.

The guy glanced down at the clipboard as he took it back, and something in his posture changed, subtle but immediate, a flicker of recognition he didn't bother hiding. "Kazehaya," he repeated, quieter now, like he was testing the weight of the name in his mouth. "That name mean something to you?"

My hand tightened on the strap of my bag, instinct more than anything else. Here we go. "Depends what you think it means," I said, keeping my tone neutral.

"Daichi Kazehaya," the guy said, setting the clipboard down carefully, both hands flat on the counter now. "He built those pods, back when Kazuhaya Tech was still around."

I nodded once, slowly, not giving anything away I didn't have to. "He was my father."

The guy's expression shifted again, warmer this time, something almost apologetic sitting underneath the recognition. "Leon," he said, offering a hand across the counter. "My sister and I run this place now. Your dad's work is one of the reasons we're still running at all. I'm sorry for your loss."

I shook his hand, grip firm but brief, and let go before my throat could tighten in a way I wasn't going to spend time processing right now. "Thanks."

Leon nodded toward the back hallway, the gesture easy, like we'd known each other longer than thirty seconds. "Pods are through there, second door on the left. Take your time, no one else is using them tonight."

• • •

The pod sat in the corner of a small, dim room that smelled like dust and old plastic, its curved shell reflecting the single overhead light in long, uneven streaks where the casing had been scratched and re-sealed too many times over the years. I stood in front of it for a moment, just taking it in, the worn edges, the faded Kazuhaya Tech logo still visible near the base, the way the whole thing looked like a relic from a different era of VR, back when full-dive was still experimental and nobody knew if it would even catch on.

Dad built this. The thought sat heavier in my chest than it should have, a mix of something like pride and something closer to grief that I wasn't going to let myself sit with right now. Alright. Let's see if I can actually make it work.

I set my bag down on the floor beside the pod, unzipped it, and pulled out the Nexus Enterprises sleeve. The access drive slid free, smaller than I'd expected, sleek black casing with a single port on one end and the HGO logo etched into the surface in fine gold lines that caught the overhead light when I turned it over in my hand. It felt expensive in a way that made me nervous just holding it.

I opened the pod's access panel, the latch popping with a soft click, and stared at the mess of connectors inside. Older tech, nothing like the clean plug-and-play setup the third-gen helmets used, just a tangle of ports and cables that hadn't been touched in years, dust settling in the gaps between them. This was going to take a minute.

The manual sat open on the floor beside me, pages marked in Dad's handwriting, notes scrawled in the margins where he'd worked through problems nobody else had bothered to solve. I scanned the diagrams, cross-referencing them against what I was looking at inside the panel, my hands already moving fast and efficient.

The override wasn't elegant. It was a bypass, forcing the pod to accept input it hadn't been designed to handle, rerouting power through secondary channels that weren't supposed to carry this kind of load. I worked quickly, hands steady despite the tension coiling through my shoulders, fingers moving through motions I'd practiced on the Haya EB a few times when it needed repairs.

If this fried the drive, I was done. I couldn't afford another one. I pushed the thought down and kept working.

The drive clicked into place with a sound that felt final, irreversible, and the pod's internal display flickered to life, text scrolling across the screen faster than I could read it, system checks running themselves in sequence.

I closed the access panel, the latch snapping shut with a sound that echoed in the small room, and stood there for a second just staring at the pod, hands still tingling from the work, adrenaline sitting hot under my ribs.

Moment of truth.

I climbed into the pod, the interior padded but worn, the cushioning compressed from years of use, and lay back as the shell hissed shut above me with a mechanical sigh I felt in my chest. The heads-up display lit up inside my visor, clean white text against black, and for a second I just focused on breathing, forcing my heart rate down, telling myself this was fine, this was going to work.

***[KAZUHAYA TECH FIRST-GENERATION VRPOD]***

***[INITIALIZING...]***

***[DRIVE DETECTED: HGO ACCESS LICENSE]***

***[WARNING: HARDWARE INCOMPATIBILITY DETECTED]***

***[OVERRIDE ENABLED]***

***[PROCEEDING...]***

The display went dark. Then the world did.

Sound returned first, a low hum building underneath everything, bass-heavy and resonant, like standing too close to a subwoofer. Then light, bright enough that my eyes watered behind the visor before the system normalized and my vision adjusted. I was standing in a white void, no walls, no floor I could see, no ceiling, just empty space stretching infinitely in every direction, the kind of nothing that should have been disorienting but somehow wasn't.

Holy shit. It worked. The grin tried to happen before I caught it. I was actually in.

Text appeared in front of me, floating at eye level, clean white letters that didn't cast shadows or reflections.

***[WELCOME TO ERYNDAL]***

***[CHARACTER CREATION WILL NOW BEGIN]***

A small bead of light drifted out of the white and settled at eye level, bobbing in place like it was too excited to hold still, and a bright, bubbly voice rang out of nowhere.

***[Hi there! I'm Lumi, your guide to Eryndal! Let's find the perfect you!]***

A mascot. Of course there was a mascot. I let out one breath through my nose, almost a laugh, and kept my eyes on the wheel.

A wheel materialized in the space in front of me, spinning slowly at first, each segment marked with a race name and a small icon rendered in fine detail. ***Human. Elf. Dwarf. Orc. Beastfolk. Goblin. Frostborn. Drakekin.*** The Goblin segment sat near the bottom, glowing a dull red compared to the brighter colors around it, and even without reading the stats I could tell it was flagged as the lowest-tier option, the joke pick nobody took seriously.

I wasn't attached to any of them. Human was the one that asked the least of me, so my hand moved toward it, ready to lock it in and get to attribute allocation, but I hesitated, my fingers hovering in the air a few inches from the wheel.

I glanced down at the Goblin segment, the dull red glow almost mocking in how unimpressive it looked compared to everything else on the wheel.

But curiosity was a hell of a thing and my hand drifted.

Just wanna see what it says.

In a moment of impulse I didn't fully understand, my hand moved and tapped the Goblin segment, just a light touch, barely a press.

The wheel lurched to a stop.

***[Oh! W-wait, that segment isn't...]***

Lumi's voice tripped over itself and stretched thin, and the bead of light shuddered at the edge of my vision.

The lights flickered.

Not just in the pod. Everywhere. The hum of power that had been building underneath everything cut off mid-note, and the white void dissolved into a weightless, disorienting black that made my stomach drop. My breath caught in my throat, one hand instinctively reaching out for something solid that wasn't there, my body trying to find purchase in a space that had no up or down anymore.

What the hell.

Sound crackled back in, distorted and wrong, and Leon's voice cut through the dark from somewhere outside the pod, muffled but clear enough to understand.

"Hey, you alright in there?"

I blinked hard, trying to clear my vision, but there was nothing to see yet, just the afterimage of the white void burned into my retinas. "Yeah," I called back, voice tight, adrenaline spiking hard enough that my hands were shaking. "I'm fine."

"Backup generator just kicked in," Leon said, closer now, right outside the pod. "System should stabilize in a second. You running anything unusual in there?"

Do I tell him? Does he even know what I did with the override? My mind raced, weighing the options. The pod's external monitor had been dark when I walked in, a dead screen Leon hadn't bothered to fix because solo sessions went unmonitored, which meant he couldn't actually see what was happening inside the character-creation process. He didn't know. He couldn't know unless I told him.

"No," I said, keeping my voice as level as I could manage, forcing the shake out of it. "Just character creation. Standard stuff."

"Alright," Leon said, footsteps retreating, boots scuffing on the floor outside. "Let me know if it kicks again. Might be the age of the hardware, these things aren't built like they were years ago."

The lights stabilized. The hum returned, lower now, steadier. The white void flickered back into existence around me, walls resolving out of nothing, and the character-creation wheel reappeared in front of me, but it wasn't spinning anymore. The bead of light was gone. Whatever Lumi had been about to say, she wasn't there to say it.

It had stopped.

The Goblin segment, the one that had been glowing dull red just a moment ago, was now lit up in bright green, pulsing faintly like it was locked in and waiting for confirmation. Text appeared beneath it, clean and definitive and completely final.

***[RACE SELECTED: GOBLIN]***

***[PROCEEDING TO ATTRIBUTE ALLOCATION...]***

I stared at the words, my breath caught somewhere between my ribs and my throat, the realization hitting me all at once like cold water.

No. No no no. That's not what I picked. I was just looking. My hand moved, reaching for the wheel, trying to go back, trying to undo whatever the power blip had just locked in, but the interface didn't respond. The wheel was gone. Only the confirmation screen remained, and beneath it, a single button glowing soft white.

***[CONTINUE]***

This can't be real. But even as I thought it, I knew.

My hands were shaking when I pressed **[CONTINUE]**, not because I wanted to, but because there was nothing else to press, no back button, no cancel, just forward into whatever this was about to become.

The screen dissolved, and a new set of numbers began resolving in front of me, floating at eye level, waiting to tell me exactly how bad this was.

**[END CHAPTER TWO]**
