# Chapter Two: Into the Game

Tatsuya stood in the middle of the apartment, phone still warm in his hand, the Nexus Enterprises text glowing against his thumb. The pickup notice sat there like a dare he’d already accepted without meaning to.

“No going back now.” He let the screen go dark and dropped the phone on the couch.

The television was still paused on London Spire’s trophy shot, confetti frozen mid-fall behind them like the world had stopped right at their peak. He grabbed the remote and flipped channels until HGO TV came up, the same feed that ran at WattsUp during his shifts, Kazuku’s official broadcast network. The bear wasn’t on-screen this time. A panel of analysts sat around a glass table instead, all of them talking fast, hands moving, graphics flashing up behind them.

“...complete roster shakeup,” one of them was saying, gesturing at something off-camera. “Teams built their entire identity around 3v3 flexibility, and now they’ve got three weeks to find specialists for roles hybrid builds used to cover. Dosuke AI’s team is already shopping for a dedicated support, and they’re not the only ones.”

Tatsuya’s hand went still on the remote. “Dosuke’s scrambling? That’s wild.” The thought landed harder than it should have. Dosuke AI’s build had always reminded him of the old Warden concept he and Eren had worked out together on graph paper, back when they’d still been theorizing builds neither of them could afford to play. A jack-of-all-trades setup that could flex into damage, healing, or crowd control depending on what the team needed. Effective in 3v3 because versatility covered gaps. 

Another analyst cut in, pulling up a graphic that filled half the screen. “And it’s not just the pros. Veteran players are cashing in their avatars already for Legacy Points en masse to rebuild fresh for the new season. The economy’s about to flip hard.”

Tatsuya muted it and tossed the remote back on the couch, one hand coming up to rub the back of his neck. “Heard enough. The whole competitive scene’s in flux, which means there’s a window. Just gotta actually get in before it closes.”

He grabbed his laptop off the kitchen counter and dropped onto the couch, the cushions sinking under him in the spot that was basically his now. He opened the HGO Companion App. Market prices loaded first, item-set data sorted by zone and rarity tier. Blue gear running five figures in yen for a single piece. Legendary gear didn’t even have listed prices, just auction-house estimates that made his stomach drop when he scrolled past them. 

He scrolled past all of it, looking for the baseline, the floor a new player could actually stand on without dropping money that was common gear cheap, uncommon attainable if you knew where to farm.

“The start point matters more than the endpoint right now. ”

He pulled up the race-select data, the per-race stat table. Six numbers running down each row. Courage, Power, Wisdom, Speed, Fortune, Energy. He’d seen versions of this table before in forums and theorycrafting threads, but those had all been guesswork and speculation. 

Elf caught his eye first, same as it always did. High Wisdom, solid Energy, average everywhere else. Magic-capable by default, which most races straight up weren’t. “Spellforger path, maybe Windrider if I want ranged DPS. Wisdom scales spell damage and learning speed, that’s the gate I actually care about.”

He scrolled further down the table, checking Dwarf, Beastfolk, Human, each one trading strengths in different directions. Dwarves traded Speed for raw Power and Courage, tanky but slow. Beastfolk leaned on Speed and Power, Tank Busters with crit potential. Humans split the difference on everything, the safe pick for someone who didn’t know what they wanted yet and didn’t want to get locked into the wrong race.

“Not me. I know exactly what I want.” He went back to Elf and stared at the Wisdom stat. “Been planning this for three years. Not changing it now.”

He closed the laptop and set it on the coffee table, leaning back against the couch with both hands behind his head. “Read enough. Can’t theory-craft my way into being good, gotta actually play it.”

Research had a ceiling. He could read market trends and stat tables until his eyes bled, but this was full-dive VR, a completely different animal from watching streams on a couch. 

He grabbed his phone off the couch, the screen lighting up with the time and his bank balance which he had a widget sitting at the top of the screen.

[¥47,320]

He ignored it and unlocked the screen and opened Loop instead. His profile loaded, follower count sitting in the corner like it always did, a number that hadn’t moved in six months.

[9 followers]

Nine people he didn’t know, following an account he barely posted from, watching a guy who’d never logged into the game they all cared about. “That’s gonna change real soon.”Once he finished scanning on loop he pulled up a cafe booking app, filtering by ward, then by availability. Third-gen cafes across Shibuya came up first, sleek storefronts with waiting lists that stretched into next year, every single slot booked solid. He scrolled past them, already knowing what he’d find, Winter Bash hype driving demand past anything the infrastructure could actually handle.

Second-gen pods weren’t much better. The Tabuchi-backed cafes had availability, technically, but the earliest slot was March, three months out. “Not waiting three months..”

He kept scrolling, deeper into results he’d already written off as dead ends, when a single listing caught his eye halfway down the third page. 

Sakura Arcade, a name he didn’t recognize, marked with a small gray tag that read [Legacy Hardware - First-Gen Pods Available]. The description mentioned limited compatibility with newer titles, walk-ins accepted, and a single line at the bottom that made him stop scrolling entirely.

[Free after 5 PM on weekdays.]

“Free?” He clicked through to the full listing.  One pod, first-generation Kazuhaya Tech, the same hardware his father built before Nexus Enterprises bought the patent rights.

“Shouldn’t run at all. Unless you know exactly what’s underneath the casing.” And he did, or close enough. Dad’s notes went back further than its early development. “Which means it’s possible. That’s all I can go off of.”

He checked the time. 3:47 PM.

“Sakura Arcade’s free window opens in just over an hour. Still gotta pick up the drive from Nexus first.” He stood, grabbed his jacket off the back of the couch where he’d left it this morning, and headed for his room, his dads old work room. He pushed the door open to a space that still smelled faintly like old solder and circuit boards, a smell that never quite left no matter how many times Momo complained about it.

Daichi’s tech manual sat on the shelf above his desk, spine cracked from years of use, pages dog-eared in a dozen places where his father had marked something worth coming back to. Tatsuya pulled it down, the weight of it familiar in his hands, and tucked it under his arm without thinking twice.

“Might need this. Just a hunch.”

Then he was out the door, down the stairs to Haya EB that was parked where he left it. He placed the helmet over his head tucking his locs in so he could pull down his visor and pressed his hand in touch screen to start it up. He kicked the stand up and pushed towards the asphalt directions navigating him towards Nexus. 

• • •

Nexus Enterprises’ flagship store took up half a block in Ginza, glass and brushed steel rising three stories over a plaza with more foot traffic than Tatsuya had seen anywhere outside a WattsUp on tournament night. He’d been down here maybe twice in his life, both times as a kid. The place didn’t look like it sold anything so much as it looked like a museum for tech people wished they could afford.

He pushed through the front doors and the smell hit him first, something clean and faintly metallic, antiseptic almost.

The ground floor read more showroom than store, third-gen helmets mounted on individual pedestals under warm spotlights, HGO’s compass rose etched into the glass casing around each one, reflections bouncing off polished floors that looked like nobody was allowed to actually walk on them. A wall-sized display looped match highlights on a screen taller than he was, Yuna’s voice cutting through the ambient noise every time the reel restarted.

“...and that’s game, London Spire takes the World Championship in a clean three-to-one...”

“Of course they’re playing that on repeat.” He kept his head down and kept moving.

The pickup counter sat toward the back, past a bank of demo pods where a handful of kids waited their turn to try on a helmet for five free minutes. A line had formed anyway, everyone holding the same Nexus Enterprises confirmation text he had on his own phone. 

Tatsuya joined the line and kept his hands in his pockets, watching the guy ahead of him collect a small package, the sleeve glossy with the Nexus logo, the compass rose stamped in gold across the front. The same package got handed over three more times before Tatsuya reached the counter himself.

“Confirmation number,” the attendant said, not looking up from his tablet.

Tatsuya rattled it off from memory: 4472-KAZE and watched the guy scan something on his screen before disappearing into the back. He came out a moment later with the same package he handed everyone else before him.,”Drive’s pre-loaded with your license,” the attendant said, still not making eye contact. “You’re good to go.”

“Appreciate it,” Tatsuya said, taking the package and putting it in his backpack, and turning back toward the door before the guy could launch into an upsell pitch about warranty extensions or premium helmets he couldn’t afford and didn’t need.

Outside, the sun was already dropping behind the buildings, late-afternoon light going orange at the edges, the kind of light that said you had maybe an hour before everything went dark. He checked his phone. 4:22 PM. Sakura Arcade’s free window opened in thirty-eight minutes, and he still had to get all the way back across town to Shibuya, navigate whatever traffic was clogging up the main routes, and hope the place actually existed and wasn’t just some abandoned storefront with a listing nobody’d bothered to take down.

He pulled his helmet on, the visor clicking into place with the sound he’d heard every morning for two years, and kicked the Haya EB to life. The engine caught on the first try, humming up through his palms the way it always did when it was actually behaving, and he opened it up down the nearest stretch of clear road, downtown thinning out into the streets he actually knew, the ones that didn’t make him feel like he was trespassing just by being there.

• • •

Sakura Arcade sat on a side street Tatsuya didn’t recognize, tucked between a shuttered ramen shop with faded kanji on the sign and a convenience store that looked like it was barely hanging on. The building itself was older than most of the surrounding blocks, brick and wood paneling where everything else had gone glass and steel, warm pendant lights glowing through the front windows in a way that read more like a lounge than a gaming cafe. 

“This is it? Doesn’t look like much.” He thought as he parked the Haya EB around the side, out of the way, and pulled his helmet off, hanging it on one handlebar, then proceeded to the door with his backpack on.

He stepped through the front door into a space that smelled faintly like old carpet and wild scents. Themed gaming chairs lined the walls in neat rows, each one facing a monitor, most of them dark and unused. 

Behind the counter, a guy in his mid-thirties looked up from a tablet, dark hair pulled back in a small knot, sleeves rolled to his elbows. 

“Can I help you?” he asked, setting the tablet down and straightening up slightly.

“I’m here for a pod,” Tatsuya said, stepping up to the counter. “First-gen, if you’ve got availability.”

The guy’s expression shifted, not quite a wince, more like someone who’d had this exact conversation too many times today and was already tired of repeating himself. “I do, but I’m gonna warn you up front that they’re first-gen Kazuhaya Tech. They don’t play well with newer titles. I’ve had to turn away a dozen HGO hopefuls since the expansion announcement hit. It’s not gonna run the way you think it will.”

“I know,” Tatsuya said, keeping his voice level, not defensive, just matter-of-fact. “That’s fine.”

The guy studied him for a second, like he was trying to figure out whether Tatsuya actually knew what he was walking into or was just saying he did to sound confident, then shrugged and pulled a clipboard off the counter, flipping it around and sliding it across with a pen. “Sign in. Name, time in. You’ve got the back room to yourself, only one pod’s actually functional, the other one’s just a shell at this point.”

Tatsuya took the pen and wrote his name across the top line in clean block letters, the same way he’d been signing forms since middle school. Tatsuya Kazehaya.

The guy glanced down at the clipboard as he took it back, and something in his posture changed, subtle but immediate, a flicker of recognition he didn’t bother hiding. “Kazehaya,” he repeated, quieter now, like he was testing the weight of the name in his mouth. “That name mean something to you?”

Tatsuya’s hand tightened on the strap of his bag, instinct more than anything else. “Here we go.” “Depends what you think it means,” he said, keeping his tone neutral.

“Daichi Kazehaya,” the guy said, setting the clipboard down carefully, both hands flat on the counter now. “He built those pods, back when Kazuhaya Tech was still around.”

Tatsuya nodded once, slowly, not giving anything away he didn’t have to. “He was my father.”

The guy’s expression shifted again, warmer this time, something almost apologetic sitting underneath the recognition. “Leon,” he said, offering a hand across the counter. “My sister and I run this place now. Your dad’s work is one of the reasons we are still running at all. I’m sorry for your loss.”

Tatsuya shook his hand, grip firm but brief, and let go before his throat could tighten in a way he wasn’t going to spend time processing right now. “Thanks.”

Leon nodded toward the back hallway, the gesture easy, like they’d known each other longer than thirty seconds. “Pods are through there, second door on the left. Take your time, no one else is using them tonight.”

• • •

The pod sat in the corner of a small, dim room that smelled like dust and old plastic, its curved shell reflecting the single overhead light in long, uneven streaks where the casing had been scratched and re-sealed too many times over the years. Tatsuya stood in front of it for a moment, just taking it in, the worn edges, the faded Kazuhaya Tech logo still visible near the base, the way the whole thing looked like a relic from a different era of VR, back when full-dive was still experimental and nobody knew if it would even catch on.

“Dad built this.” The thought sat heavier in his chest than it should have, a mix of something like pride and something closer to grief that he wasn’t going to let himself sit with right now. “Alright. Let’s see if I can actually make it work.”

He set his bag down on the floor beside the pod, unzipped it, and pulled out the Nexus Enterprises sleeve. The access drive slid free, smaller than he’d expected, sleek black casing with a single port on one end and the HGO logo etched into the surface in fine gold lines that caught the overhead light when he turned it over in his hand. It felt expensive in a way that made him nervous just holding it.

He opened the pod’s access panel, the latch popping with a soft click, and stared at the mess of connectors inside. Older tech, nothing like the clean plug-and-play setup the third-gen helmets used, just a tangle of ports and cables that hadn’t been touched in years, dust settling in the gaps between them. “This is gonna take a minute.”

The manual sat open on the floor beside him, pages marked in his father’s handwriting, notes scrawled in the margins where Daichi had worked through problems nobody else had bothered to solve. Tatsuya scanned the diagrams, cross-referencing them against what he was looking at inside the panel, his hands already moving  fast and efficient.

The override wasn’t elegant. It was a bypass, forcing the pod to accept input it hadn’t been designed to handle, rerouting power through secondary channels that weren’t supposed to carry this kind of load. He worked quickly, hands steady despite the tension coiling through his shoulders, fingers moving through motions he’d practiced on the Haya EB a few times when it needed repaired.

“If this fries the drive, I’m done. Can’t afford another one.” He pushed the thought down and kept working.

The drive clicked into place with a sound that felt final, irreversible, and the pod’s internal display flickered to life, text scrolling across the screen faster than he could read it, system checks running themselves in sequence.

Tatsuya closed the access panel, the latch snapping shut with a sound that echoed in the small room, and stood there for a second just staring at the pod, hands still tingling from the work, adrenaline sitting hot under his ribs.

“Moment of truth.”

He climbed into the pod, the interior padded but worn, the cushioning compressed from years of use, and lay back as the shell hissed shut above him with a mechanical sigh he felt in his chest. The heads-up display lit up inside his visor, clean white text against black, and for a second he just focused on breathing, forcing his heart rate down, telling himself this was fine, this was going to work.

**[KAZUHAYA TECH FIRST-GENERATION VRPOD]**

**[INITIALIZING...]**

**[DRIVE DETECTED: HGO ACCESS LICENSE]**

**[WARNING: HARDWARE INCOMPATIBILITY DETECTED]**

**[OVERRIDE ENABLED]**

**[PROCEEDING...]**

The display went dark. Then the world did.

Sound returned first, a low hum building underneath everything, bass-heavy and resonant, like standing too close to a subwoofer. Then light, bright enough that Tatsuya’s eyes watered behind the visor before the system normalized and his vision adjusted. He was standing in a white void, no walls, no floor he could see, no ceiling, just empty space stretching infinitely in every direction, the kind of nothing that should have been disorienting but somehow wasn’t.

“Holy shit. It worked.” The grin tried to happen before he caught it. “Im Actually in.!!”

Text appeared in front of him, floating at eye level, clean white letters that didn’t cast shadows or reflections.

**[WELCOME TO ERYNDAL]**

**[CHARACTER CREATION WILL NOW BEGIN]**

A wheel materialized in the space in front of him, spinning slowly at first, each segment marked with a race name and a small icon rendered in fine detail. **Human. Elf. Dwarf. Orc. Beastfolk. Goblin. Frostborn. Drakekin.** The Goblin segment sat near the bottom, glowing a dull red compared to the brighter colors around it, and even without reading the stats he could tell it was flagged as the lowest-tier option, the joke pick nobody took seriously.

“Elf. That’s the play. Knew that before I even walked in here.” His hand moved toward the Elf segment, ready to lock it in and move on to the actual important part, attribute allocation, but something made him hesitate, his fingers hovering in the air a few inches from the wheel.

He glanced down at the Goblin segment, the dull red glow almost mocking in how unimpressive it looked compared to everything else on the wheel. 

But curiosity was a hell of a thing and his hand drifted.

 “Just wanna see what it says.”

In a moment of impulse he didn’t fully understand, his hand moved and tapped the Goblin segment, just a light touch, barely a press.

The wheel lurched to a stop.

The lights flickered.

Not just in the pod. Everywhere. The hum of power that had been building underneath everything cut off mid-note, and the white void dissolved into a weightless, disorienting black that made his stomach drop. Tatsuya’s breath caught in his throat, one hand instinctively reaching out for something solid that wasn’t there, his body trying to find purchase in a space that had no up or down anymore.

“What the hell.”

Sound crackled back in, distorted and wrong, and Leon’s voice cut through the dark from somewhere outside the pod, muffled but clear enough to understand.

“Hey, you alright in there?”

Tatsuya blinked hard, trying to clear his vision, but there was nothing to see yet, just the afterimage of the white void burned into his retinas. “Yeah,” he called back, voice tight, adrenaline spiking hard enough that his hands were shaking. “I’m fine.”

“Backup generator just kicked in,” Leon said, closer now, right outside the pod. “System should stabilize in a second. You running anything unusual in there?”

“Do I tell him? Does he even know what I did with the override?” Tatsuya’s mind raced, weighing the options. The pod’s external monitor had been dark when he’d walked in, a dead screen Leon hadn’t bothered to fix because solo sessions went unmonitored, which meant Leon couldn’t actually see what was happening inside the character-creation process. “He doesn’t know. Can’t know unless I tell him.”

“No,” Tatsuya said, keeping his voice as level as he could manage, forcing the shake out of it. “Just character creation. Standard stuff.”

“Alright,” Leon said, footsteps retreating, boots scuffing on the floor outside. “Let me know if it kicks again. Might be the age of the hardware, these things aren’t built like there were years ago”

The lights stabilized. The hum returned, lower now, steadier. The white void flickered back into existence around him, walls resolving out of nothing, and the character-creation wheel reappeared in front of him, but it wasn’t spinning anymore.

It had stopped.

The Goblin segment, the one that had been glowing dull red just a moment ago, was now lit up in bright green, pulsing faintly like it was locked in and waiting for confirmation. Text appeared beneath it, clean and definitive and completely final.

**[RACE SELECTED: GOBLIN]**

**[PROCEEDING TO ATTRIBUTE ALLOCATION...]**

Tatsuya stared at the words, his breath caught somewhere between his ribs and his throat, the realization hitting him all at once like cold water.

“No. No no no. That’s not what I picked. I was just looking.” His hand moved, reaching for the wheel, trying to go back, trying to undo whatever the power blip had just locked in, but the interface didn’t respond. The wheel was gone. Only the confirmation screen remained, and beneath it, a single button glowing soft white.

**[CONTINUE]**

“This can’t be real..” But even as he thought it, he knew. 

His hands were shaking when he pressed **[CONTINUE]**, not because he wanted to, but because there was nothing else to press, no back button, no cancel, just forward into whatever this was about to become.

The screen dissolved, and a new set of numbers began resolving in front of him, floating at eye level, waiting to tell him exactly how bad this was.

**[END CHAPTER TWO]**
