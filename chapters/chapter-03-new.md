# Chapter Three: Into the Game

I woke up wrong, sluggish and heavy, and I knew it before I opened my eyes. Sunlight instead of the dim pre-dawn gray I should have woken to, warm on my eyelids in a way that said it was way past morning. My body didn't do sunlight like this, not after a night shift, not without a fight.

My phone was still in my hand, the screen dark and cool against my palm. I thumbed it awake: **3:04 PM**.

*"No. No, no, no."* I was off the couch before I was fully awake, the blanket sliding to the floor, my heart already going. The television was still paused on the soldiers marching through the gate. Momo's door stood open and her room was empty, the bed made in the quick, lopsided way she did it on her way out. Taji's side of the couch was cold.

*"Chiyo is going to kill me. How did I sleep through the whole day?"* The sale clock had been running the entire time, a fifth of its seventy-two hours already gone, and I hadn't even picked up the drive. I stood in the middle of the living room with my phone in one hand, listening for anybody at all, and heard the fridge hum.

A note sat on the kitchen counter, folded once, my name across the front in Chiyo's handwriting. I grabbed it too fast and had to read the first line twice.

**[Left early with the twins for the Kyoto trip, didn't want to wake you after the night you had. We'll be back in six days. Please eat something real, not just convenience store rice balls. Chiyo]**

I set the note flat on the counter and smoothed the fold. *"The trip. Right. The trip."* It had been on the calendar stuck to the fridge for two weeks, and I'd still lost track of it. Nobody was waiting on me. Nobody was angry. The only thing I'd missed was sleep I clearly needed, and the rice balls line was pure Chiyo. I read it once more before I put it down. Six days with nobody home but me.

My phone buzzed in my hand, then again. A text from an unknown number sat at the top of my notifications, sender marked **Nexus Enterprises**.

**[Your HGO Access Drive order has been processed and is ready for pickup. Please collect within 24 hours of this notification. Pickup Code: 4472-KAZE]**

That settled it. The dread from the morning finally had somewhere to go. *"Okay. I need to go pick this up. Today, before anything else goes wrong."* I killed the television before I could look at it any longer. Corporate could run HGO on every screen at the store, but not in my living room.

I took my laptop off the kitchen counter, dropped onto the couch, and opened the HGO Companion App. WattsUp made every one of us put it on our phones in case a customer asked about the game, and I'd told myself that was the only reason it was there. It wasn't. I'd had it open most mornings since the beta started, my thumb always ready to close it. I hated how much of it I knew.

The feed opened on Saturday's headline. **[Dosuke AI's group cut one step short of the final. Role award for the season.]** *"He deserved better than a Saturday exit."* Dosuke was the one player I'd actually sat and watched, and his build looked far too much like the one Eren and I used to draw on our tablets. I'd never said that to anybody. I scratched at my goatee, then tapped over to the market.

The auction board was thinner than I'd expected. No shelves of common gear, no flood of sellers. Closed-beta characters were frozen at the lock, everything they'd earned had turned into legacy items, and legacy items were unique. Players could still drop them and sell them, and that was about all that was for sale: single pieces nobody else would ever own a copy of, most of them off last night's fight, running into the hundreds of thousands of yen, a few with no price at all, just auction estimates with more zeroes than my whole bank balance.

Then I saw what it cost to sell anything. Every listing carried a posting fee up front and another cut when it sold, and on a six-figure piece that cut was a number I could feel in my own account. *"The tax eats most of the margin before the seller ever sees it."* Nothing said anybody had to use the board. Players could trade straight to each other, if they trusted each other, if they knew who to trade with. I filed that away for later, the way I filed away anything that looked like a gap, and then I let it go, because I didn't have anything to sell yet.

*"So that's where the money is."* There was real money moving through this game, and I needed some of it. Crownfall's ten million yen was the only pool big enough, and nobody got paid for showing up. You got paid for winning, or for getting close enough that people noticed. I couldn't walk in as a nobody with a random character. I needed a strong build, something balanced enough to be productive from the first hour.

The race table sat on the same page, six numbers down each row. Courage, Power, Wisdom, Speed, Fortune, Energy. I opened the Elf row without really deciding to. Wisdom stacked high, Spellforger on top of it, control and burst, the build I'd spent months drawing out with Eren and had never once been able to play. Eren had a copy now with his name on it, and Haruto had looked straight through it last night. *"I'd just like to see who the brain behind that build looks like when it's actually playing it."* Eren had the build. He didn't know how to run it, and the build never opened up for anybody who didn't.

*"Let's see what the original does."* I caught myself grinning at the screen and checked the empty room to make sure nobody had seen, half excitement and half nerves. Dad's game, the world he said he helped create, and I was about to stand inside it with a build I actually believed in.

I shut the laptop and set it on the coffee table. *"Six days."* Chiyo would be back in six days, and I wanted to have something to show her by then. *"I've got the build. I've never played a minute of it."* I was sitting on the very edge of the cushion, and I made myself lean back.

My bank balance sat on my phone as a widget at the top of the screen.

**[¥47,320]**

It was mine, but not all of it. I paid what I could toward groceries and a small bill Chiyo had handed me so I'd have something to carry, and she covered the rest. She owned the hotels, she could have covered every bit of it, and I'd stopped questioning it a while back. It made sense.

I opened Loop. My profile loaded, the follower count in the corner a number that hadn't moved in six months.

**[9 followers]**

Nine people I didn't know, following an account I barely posted from. I closed it and pulled up the cafe booking app, filtering by ward, then by availability. The sale and the open beta had every cafe in the city booked solid, third-gen cafes across Shibuya with waiting lists stretching into next year.

Second-gen cafes ran pods built by developers the Tabuchis had put money into. The developers hadn't done anything to me, and I still wasn't putting a yen into anything the Tabuchis stood behind. It didn't matter anyway, the earliest opening was March, three months out. *"Three months. Mom's bills don't wait three months."* It was the same reason I'd never gone deep into other VR. Every mainstream game on every platform ran on second-gen, and the old first-gen stuff was a few retro games I'd played back when I had the time, and I never had the time anymore. I scrolled past the last result with the sale clock ticking in the corner of the screen, rubbing two fingers along my goatee. I went deeper into the listings anyway.

A listing halfway down the third page stopped me. Sakura Arcade, a name I didn't know, with a small gray tag reading **[Legacy Hardware - First-Gen Pods Available]**. Walk-ins accepted, limited compatibility with newer titles, and a single line at the bottom.

**[Free after 5 PM on weekdays.]**

*"Free?"* Something small and stupid lifted in my chest, the first thing all afternoon that wasn't dread, and I shut it down before it could get comfortable. It was probably a typo. I clicked through anyway. One pod, first-generation Kazuhaya Tech, the same hardware my father built before Nexus Enterprises moved on to the third-gen helmets. First-gen pods were basically extinct, hardware that ran a couple of early VR games and not much else. There was no reason one should run HGO. Maybe it could, if Dad's manual got me lucky. Maybe I'd cross town and find a pod that did nothing but power on. It was a gamble, and it was the only one I could afford.

I checked the time. 3:47 PM. The window opened in just over an hour, I still had to pick up the drive from Nexus first, and the EB hadn't held a charge since last night. I changed out of the WattsUp shirt I'd slept in, pulled on jeans and my jacket, shoved my feet into my boots, and went down the hall to my room, the corner my father had worked out of for most of my life, the smell of old solder and cold circuit boards hitting me at the door, the smell Momo had complained about for years.

I wasn't leaving the manual on that shelf anymore. I pulled it down, spine cracked, pages dog-eared in a dozen places, the letter still hidden where I'd tucked it in the controller diagrams, and I left it right there. I flipped to the controller board section until a note in his slanted handwriting stopped my thumb. **Joint on rail 3 goes cold under load. Reflow.**

*"There you are."* I took his iron, the flux and solder, his meter, and the bin of spare parts from under the desk, rolled it all into his canvas tool roll, tucked the manual under my arm, and took the stairs down to the EB.

It was parked where I'd left it, black paint still clean, dash dead. I propped it on its stand, pulled the cover off the controller board, and ran the meter along the power rail before I touched anything. The reading sagged under load exactly the way the margin said it would. That was the part that had always come easy, you didn't guess, you measured and let the board show you where it hurt. It was the reason Tochi had let me in on merit, and the only part of that place I'd never had to defend. A hairline crack in the joint on rail 3, hardly visible, enough to cut power the second the motor pulled hard. I reflowed it, found a second joint starting to go next to it and redid that too, then swapped the worn dash connector for one from Dad's bin, matched pin by pin until it seated with a click. It took twenty minutes, the cold air biting the back of my neck, and I didn't breathe easy until I pressed my thumb to the screen.

The dash took its time reading my print, that half-second of scanning to confirm who was riding, and then it lit green. The visor on the helmet threw a bright route line across the street. *"Good. Good. We're good."* I let my forehead drop against the handlebar for one second, long enough to breathe out. The EB looked and ran better than it had in weeks, and I had Dad's spares to thank for it. The bin was a lot lighter than when I'd started, though. *"If I want to keep this thing alive past the winter, I'm going to need parts I can't pay for with forty-seven thousand yen."* It was fixed enough for today.

I clipped the manual into the rear case, pulled the helmet on, tucked my locs in so I could drop the visor, and rode out toward Ginza with the route line leading the way.

• • •

Nexus Enterprises' flagship store took up half a block, glass and brushed steel rising three stories over a plaza crowded with more foot traffic than I'd seen anywhere outside a WattsUp on a holiday weekend. I'd been down here maybe twice in my life, both times as a kid, and both times Dad had held my hand through the doors.

I pushed through them, and the air was cold and clean, with a faint smell of new plastic and floor polish.

The ground floor read more showroom than store. Third-gen helmets sat on individual pedestals under warm spotlights, HGO's compass rose etched into the glass casing around each one, the floors polished to the point where I felt bad stepping on them. A wall-sized display looped highlights from last night's battle, Yuna's voice cutting through the room every time the reel restarted.

"...Thornwatch is London's, and London Spire takes Season Zero..."

*"Of course they're playing that on repeat."* I kept my head down and kept moving.

The pickup counter sat toward the back, past a row of demo pods where a handful of kids waited their turn to try a helmet for five free minutes. A line had formed anyway, everyone holding the same confirmation text I had on my phone. I joined it with my hands in my pockets and watched the guy ahead of me collect a small glossy sleeve with the compass rose stamped in gold. The same sleeve got handed over three more times before I reached the counter, and every one of them took longer than the clock on my phone could spare.

"Confirmation number," the attendant said, not looking up from his tablet.

I rattled it off from memory, 4472-KAZE, and he disappeared into the back and came out with the same sleeve he'd handed everyone else.

"Drive's pre-loaded with your license. You're good to go."

"Appreciate it," I said, and dropped the sleeve into my backpack and turned for the door before he could pitch me a warranty extension or a premium helmet I couldn't afford.

Outside, the sun was dropping behind the buildings, the light going orange at the edges. I checked my phone. 4:51 PM. Sakura Arcade's window opened in nine minutes, I still had to get across town, and the place might turn out to be an abandoned storefront with a listing nobody had taken down. *"If it's a dead end, I just burned the whole afternoon."* I got on the EB before I could talk myself out of it.

The visor clicked into place with the sound I'd heard every morning for two years, and the EB caught on the first try, the hum climbing through my palms the way it did when it was behaving. I took it down the nearest clear stretch of road, downtown thinning out into the streets I actually knew.

• • •

Sakura Arcade sat on a side street I didn't recognize, wedged between a closed ramen shop with faded kanji on the sign and a convenience store that looked like it was hanging on by a thread. The building was older than the blocks around it, brick and wood paneling where everything else had gone glass and steel, warm pendant lights glowing through the front windows.

*"This is it? Doesn't look like much."* I parked the EB around the side, hung the helmet on one handlebar, and went to the door with my backpack on.

Inside, it smelled like old carpet and something floral. Themed gaming chairs lined the walls in neat rows, each facing a monitor, most of them dark.

Behind the counter, a guy in his mid-thirties looked up from a tablet, dark hair pulled back in a small knot, sleeves rolled to his elbows.

"Can I help you?"

"I'm here for a pod," I said, stepping up. "First-gen, if you've got availability."

His expression shifted, not quite a wince, more like someone who'd had this exact conversation too many times today. "I do, but I'll warn you up front, they're first-gen Kazuhaya Tech. They don't play well with newer titles. I've turned away a dozen HGO hopefuls since the sale hit. It's not gonna run the way you think it will."

"I know," I said, level, not defensive. "That's fine."

He studied me a second, then shrugged and slid a clipboard across with a pen. "Sign in. Name, time in. You've got the back room to yourself. Only one pod's actually functional, the other one's a shell at this point."

I wrote my name across the top line in the same clean block letters I'd used since middle school. Tatsuya Kazehaya.

He glanced at the clipboard as he took it back, and something in his posture changed, quick but obvious, a flicker of recognition he didn't bother hiding. "Kazehaya," he said, quieter. "That name mean something to you?"

My hand tightened on my bag strap. *"Here we go."* "Depends what you think it means."

"Daichi Kazehaya." He set the clipboard down carefully, both hands flat on the counter. "He built those pods, back when Kazuhaya Tech was still around."

I nodded once, slowly. "He was my father."

His face softened, something almost apologetic under the recognition. "Leon," he said, offering a hand across the counter. "My sister and I run this place now. Your dad's work is a big reason we're still running at all. I'm sorry for your loss."

I shook it, firm and brief, and let go before my throat could do anything I wasn't going to deal with right now. "Thanks."

Leon nodded toward a back hallway, easy about it. "Pods are through there, second door on the left. Take your time. No one else is using them tonight."

• • •

The pod sat in the corner of a small, dim room that smelled like dust and old plastic. One overhead light caught the curved shell in long, uneven streaks where the casing had been scratched and resealed too many times. I stood in front of it a moment, taking in the worn edges and the faded Kazuhaya Tech logo near the base, a relic from the days when full-dive was still experimental and nobody knew if it would catch on.

*"Dad built this."* It sat heavier in my chest than I wanted, pride and grief tangled together, and I wasn't going to look at it right now. *"Alright. Let's see if I can make it work."*

I dropped my bag beside the pod and pulled the sleeve out. The access drive slid free, smaller than I'd expected, black with a single port and the HGO logo etched in thin gold lines that flashed when I turned it in the light. It felt expensive enough to make me nervous holding it.

The pod's access panel opened with a soft click on a mess of old connectors, nothing like the plug-and-play of the third-gen helmets, just a tangle of ports and cables that hadn't been touched in years, dust packed in the gaps. This was going to take a minute.

I opened the manual on the floor beside me. Dad's handwriting crowded the margins, notes from problems nobody else had bothered to solve, and I cross-referenced the diagrams against the panel with my hands already moving. The manual got me the bypass. It didn't get me everything. Whole sections of the harness were missing, pages he'd never written or someone had taken, and I wrote down the gaps in my head for later.

The override wasn't elegant. It forced the pod to accept input it hadn't been built to handle, rerouting power through secondary channels that weren't made for the load, and nothing in the manual promised it would work. The first try threw a red error and the display died. I sat back on my heels and listened to the pod tick as it cooled, reread Dad's margin note, found the connection I'd crossed, and tried again, the same careful motions I'd used on the EB. *"Come on. It has to take."*

*"If this fries the drive, I'm done. I can't afford another one."*

The drive clicked into place with a sound that felt final, and the pod's internal display flickered on, text scrolling too fast to read, system checks running themselves. I snapped the panel shut and stood there with my hands tingling from the work. *"Moment of truth."*

I climbed in, the padding worn and compressed from years of use, and lay back as the shell hissed shut above me. The heads-up display lit inside my visor, white text on black. I told myself to slow down my breathing and let the numbers settle.

***[KAZUHAYA TECH FIRST-GENERATION VRPOD]***

***[INITIALIZING...]***

***[DRIVE DETECTED: HGO ACCESS LICENSE]***

***[WARNING: HARDWARE INCOMPATIBILITY DETECTED]***

***[OVERRIDE ENABLED]***

***[PROCEEDING...]***

The display went dark. Then the world did.

Sound came first, a low hum building underneath everything, bass-heavy, loud enough that I felt it in my teeth. Then light, bright enough that my eyes watered before the system adjusted. I was standing in a white void, no walls, no floor I could see, just empty space in every direction, the kind of nothing that should have been disorienting and wasn't.

*"Holy shit. It worked."* My shoulders dropped for the first time since I'd woken up, and the grin tried to happen before I caught it. I was actually in.

Text floated at eye level, clean white letters with no shadows.

***[WELCOME TO ERYNDAL]***

***[CHARACTER CREATION WILL NOW BEGIN]***

A small bead of light drifted out of the white and settled in front of me, bobbing in place, and a bright, bubbly voice rang out of nowhere.

***[Hi there! I'm Lumi, your guide to Eryndal! Let's find the perfect you!]***

*"Another mascot. Of course."* I huffed out something close to a laugh and kept my eyes on the bead.

The bead bobbed higher, plainly pleased with itself, and kept talking.

***[First, a little about where you're going! Hero's Glory Online is a sandbox, so a lot of it is built by the people in it. Everything you earn stays with you, the people you meet remember what you do, and nothing resets when you log off.]***

I knew all of it. Taji had recited it at the breakfast table like scripture, and I'd tuned out every word. I shifted my weight on the nothing under my boots and let her keep going.

Lumi swirled in a lazy circle, trailing sparks, and her voice dropped like she was sharing a secret.

***[Eryndal is watched over by five patrons! Each one favors a people and gifts them something no one else gets. Seranis, goddess of creation, watches over the demi-humans. The others watch over the elves, the humans and the dwarves. And the fifth? Their gifts come with a price, so most adventurers keep their distance!]***

*"Five gods, and one of them's bad news. Eren would've had his pick memorized by now."* The old plastic of the pod squeaked against my jacket, and I decided I could care about it after I'd picked a face.

The bead bounced once, like the whole speech had been rehearsed for me alone.

***[Choose your race, and the patron who favors it will bless you. Ready? Let's begin!]***

A wheel formed in front of me, spinning slowly, each segment marked with a race name and a small icon. ***Human. Elf. Dwarf. Orc. Beastfolk. Goblin. Frostborn. Drakekin.*** The Goblin segment sat near the bottom, glowing a dull red next to the brighter colors, and even without reading the stats I could tell it was the lowest-tier option, the joke pick nobody took seriously.

I knew which one I wanted before I'd finished looking. The Elf segment sat near the top, bright and clean, the build I'd been carrying around for months. My hand was already moving, and I dragged my fingers across the wheel to spin it around, scanning past the others in a hurry, past Orc, past Beastfolk, and my fingertip skimmed the dull red Goblin segment without a second thought.

The wheel lurched to a stop.

***[Oh! W-wait, that segment isn't...]***

Lumi's voice tripped over itself and stretched thin, and the bead shuddered at the edge of my vision.

The lights flickered.

Not just in the pod, everywhere. The hum cut off mid-note, and the white void dissolved into a weightless black that made my stomach drop. My hand shot out for something solid that wasn't there, my body hunting for an up or a down that didn't exist anymore.

*"What the hell."*

Sound crackled back in, warped and wrong, and Leon's voice cut through the dark from outside the pod, muffled but clear.

"Hey, you alright in there?"

I blinked hard, but there was nothing to see except the afterimage of the white void. "Yeah," I called back, voice tight. "I'm fine."

"Backup generator just kicked in," Leon said, closer now, right outside the shell. "System should stabilize in a second. You running anything unusual in there?"

*"Do I tell him? Does he even know about the override?"* The pod's external monitor had been dark when I walked in, a dead screen Leon had never bothered to fix because solo sessions went unmonitored, which meant he couldn't see anything that was happening in character creation. He didn't know, and he couldn't know unless I told him.

"No," I said, as level as I could make it. "Just character creation. Standard stuff."

"Alright." His boots scuffed away. "Let me know if it kicks again. Might be the age of the hardware, these things aren't built like they used to be."

The lights stabilized, and the hum came back lower and steadier. The white void flickered into place around me, walls resolving out of nothing, and the character-creation wheel reappeared, but it wasn't spinning anymore. The bead of light was gone. Whatever Lumi had been about to say, she wasn't there to say it.

The Goblin segment, the one that had been glowing dull red a moment ago, now burned bright green, pulsing faintly, locked in and waiting for me. Text appeared beneath it, clean and final.

***[RACE SELECTED: GOBLIN]***

***[Now, which of the five will you ch—]***

The line broke off mid-word, the letters fraying at the edge like a bad signal. I stared at the empty spot where Lumi's bead had hung.

***[PATRON ASSIGNED BY DEFAULT: SERANIS, GODDESS OF THE DEMI-HUMANS]***

*"I didn't pick a goddess either. Apparently the game picked one for me."*

***[PROCEEDING TO ATTRIBUTE ALLOCATION...]***

I stared at the words, and they didn't change no matter how many times I read them.

*"No. No no no. That's not what I picked. I wanted the Elf."* I reached for the wheel, trying to go back, trying to undo whatever the power blip had locked in, but the interface didn't respond. The wheel was gone. Only the confirmation screen remained, and under it, one button glowing soft white.

***[CONTINUE]***

*"This can't be real."* Even as I thought it, I knew it was.

I pressed **[CONTINUE]**, not because I wanted to, but because there was nothing else to press, no back button, no cancel, just forward into whatever this was about to become.

The screen dissolved, and a new set of numbers began to form in front of me, floating at eye level, waiting to tell me exactly how bad this was.

**[END CHAPTER THREE]**
