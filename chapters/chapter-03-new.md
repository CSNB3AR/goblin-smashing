# Chapter Three: Into the Game

I woke up wrong, sluggish and heavy, and I knew it before I opened my eyes. Sunlight instead of the dim pre-dawn gray I should have woken to, warm on my eyelids in a way that said it was way past morning. My body didn't do sunlight like this, not after a night shift, not without a fight.

My phone was still in my hand, the screen dark and cool against my palm. I thumbed it awake: **3:04 PM**.

*"What the hell."* I was off the couch before the thought finished, the blanket sliding to the floor, the television still paused on the soldiers marching through the gate. Momo's door stood open, her room empty, the bed made in the quick, lopsided way that meant she'd done it on her way out. Taji's side of the couch was cold.

Nobody home but me, and a good fifth of my seventy-two-hour window already gone.

*"Slept through the whole damn morning. Great start."*

A note sat on the kitchen counter, folded once, my name written across the front in Chiyo's handwriting.

**[Left early with the twins for the Kyoto trip, didn't want to wake you after the night you had. We'll be back in six days. Please eat something real, not just convenience store rice balls. Chiyo]**

Right. The trip. It had been on the calendar stuck to the fridge for two weeks, and I'd still managed to lose track of it.

My phone buzzed in my hand, then again. A text from an unknown number sat at the top of my notifications, sender marked **Nexus Enterprises**.

**[Your HGO Access Drive order has been processed and is ready for pickup. Please collect within 72 hours of this notification. Pickup Code: 4472-KAZE]**

Whatever came next, it started today, and I was already behind. I killed the television before I could look at it any longer. Corporate could run HGO on every screen at the store, but not in my living room.

I took my laptop off the kitchen counter, dropped onto the couch, and opened the HGO Companion App. I wasn't there for the game. I was there for the market. Item sets loaded sorted by zone and rarity, blue gear running five figures in yen for a single piece, legendary gear with no price at all, just auction estimates that made my stomach drop when I scrolled past them.

The sale had pulled a flood of veterans cashing out their avatars for Legacy Points before the lock, and the numbers told me what the ad had. There was real money moving through this game, and a new character with nothing could sit right at the edge of it. I scrolled until I found the floor a newcomer could stand on without paying to be there. Common gear cheap, uncommon attainable if you knew where to farm.

The race table sat on the same page, six numbers down each row. Courage, Power, Wisdom, Speed, Fortune, Energy. I'd seen versions of that table in forums and in shared tablet notes, back when Eren and I argued over which build would break the game. I closed the tab before I got to the Elf row. *"That's Eren's build now. I'm not touching it."* Human, probably. Humans split the difference on everything, the safe pick for someone who didn't want to get locked into the wrong race.

I shut the laptop and set it on the coffee table. *"I'm not trying to be good at this game. I'm trying to get paid by it."* Nothing on a page was going to tell me how that worked. I had to go in and find out.

My bank balance sat on my phone as a widget at the top of the screen.

**[¥47,320]**

I ignored it and opened Loop. My profile loaded, the follower count in the corner a number that hadn't moved in six months.

**[9 followers]**

Nine people I didn't know, following an account I barely posted from. I closed it and pulled up the cafe booking app, filtering by ward, then by availability. Third-gen cafes across Shibuya came up first, waiting lists stretching into next year, every slot booked. The sale had driven demand past anything the cafes could handle.

Second-gen pods weren't much better. The Tabuchi-backed cafes had openings, technically, but the earliest was March, three months out. *"Not waiting three months."* I scrolled deeper into results I'd already written off.

A listing halfway down the third page stopped me. Sakura Arcade, a name I didn't know, with a small gray tag reading **[Legacy Hardware - First-Gen Pods Available]**. Walk-ins accepted, limited compatibility with newer titles, and a single line at the bottom.

**[Free after 5 PM on weekdays.]**

*"Free?"* I clicked through. One pod, first-generation Kazuhaya Tech, the same hardware my father built before Nexus Enterprises moved on to the third-gen helmets. It shouldn't run HGO at all, not unless you knew what was underneath the casing, and I knew, or close enough. That was all I had to go on, but it was something.

I checked the time. 3:47 PM. The window opened in just over an hour, and I still had to pick up the drive from Nexus first. I grabbed my jacket from the back of the couch and went down the hall to Dad's old work room, the smell of old solder and circuit boards hitting me at the door, the smell Momo had complained about for years.

The manual sat on the shelf above the desk, spine cracked, pages dog-eared in a dozen places. I pulled it down, and the letter stayed where I'd tucked it, hidden in the controller diagrams. I left it there and flipped to the controller board section, my thumb running down the margins until a note in his slanted handwriting stopped it. **Joint on rail 3 goes cold under load. Reflow.**

*"There you are."* I took his iron off the shelf, tucked the manual under my arm, and went down to the EB.

It was parked where I'd left it, black paint still clean, dead dash and all. I propped it on its stand and pulled the cover off the controller board, the cold air biting the back of my neck, and found the joint on rail 3 exactly where the margin said. A hairline crack, hardly visible, enough to cut power the second the motor pulled hard. It took twenty minutes with the iron, a rag and a lot of patience, and I didn't breathe easy until I pressed my thumb to the screen.

The dash took its time reading my print, that half-second of scanning to confirm who was riding, and then it lit green. The visor on the helmet threw a bright route line across the street. *"Good. Good. We're good."* I ran a hand along the seat. It wasn't fixed forever, but it was fixed enough.

I clipped the manual into the rear case, pulled the helmet on, tucked my locs in so I could drop the visor, and rode out toward Ginza with the route line leading the way.

• • •

Nexus Enterprises' flagship store took up half a block, glass and brushed steel rising three stories over a plaza crowded with more foot traffic than I'd seen anywhere outside a WattsUp on a holiday weekend. I'd been down here maybe twice in my life, both times as a kid, and both times Dad had held my hand through the doors.

I pushed through them, and the air was cold and clean, with a faint smell of new plastic and floor polish.

The ground floor read more showroom than store. Third-gen helmets sat on individual pedestals under warm spotlights, HGO's compass rose etched into the glass casing around each one, the floors polished to the point where I felt bad stepping on them. A wall-sized display looped highlights from last night's battle, Yuna's voice cutting through the room every time the reel restarted.

"...Thornwatch is London's, and London Spire takes Season Zero..."

*"Of course they're playing that on repeat."* I kept my head down and kept moving.

The pickup counter sat toward the back, past a row of demo pods where a handful of kids waited their turn to try a helmet for five free minutes. A line had formed anyway, everyone holding the same confirmation text I had on my phone. I joined it with my hands in my pockets and watched the guy ahead of me collect a small glossy sleeve with the compass rose stamped in gold. The same sleeve got handed over three more times before I reached the counter.

"Confirmation number," the attendant said, not looking up from his tablet.

I rattled it off from memory, 4472-KAZE, and he disappeared into the back and came out with the same sleeve he'd handed everyone else.

"Drive's pre-loaded with your license. You're good to go."

"Appreciate it," I said, and dropped the sleeve into my backpack and turned for the door before he could pitch me a warranty extension or a premium helmet I couldn't afford.

Outside, the sun was dropping behind the buildings, the light going orange at the edges. I checked my phone. 4:51 PM. Sakura Arcade's window opened in nine minutes, I still had to get across town, and the place might turn out to be an abandoned storefront with a listing nobody had taken down.

The visor clicked into place with the sound I'd heard every morning for two years, and the EB caught on the first try, the hum climbing through my palms the way it did when it was behaving. I took it down the nearest clear stretch of road, downtown thinning out into the streets I actually knew.

• • •

Sakura Arcade sat on a side street I didn't recognize, wedged between a closed ramen shop with faded kanji on the sign and a convenience store that looked like it was hanging on by a thread. The building was older than the blocks around it, brick and wood paneling where everything else had gone glass and steel, warm pendant lights glowing through the front windows.

*"This is it? Doesn't look like much."* I parked the EB around the side, hung the helmet on one handlebar, and went to the door with my backpack on.

Inside, it smelled like old carpet and something floral. Themed gaming chairs lined the walls in neat rows, each facing a monitor, most of them dark.

Behind the counter, a guy in his mid-thirties looked up from a tablet, dark hair pulled back in a small knot, sleeves rolled to his elbows.

"Can I help you?"

"I'm here for a pod," I said, stepping up. "First-gen, if you've got availability."

His expression shifted, not quite a wince, more like someone who'd had this exact conversation too many times today. "I do, but I'll warn you up front, they're first-gen Kazuhaya Tech. They don't play well with newer titles. I've turned away a dozen HGO hopefuls since the sale hit. It's not gonna run the way you think it will."

"I know," I said, level, not defensive. "That's fine."

He studied me a second, then shrugged and slid a clipboard across with a pen. "Sign in. Name, time in. You've got the back room to yourself. Only one pod's actually functional, the other one's a shell at this point."

I wrote my name across the top line in the same clean block letters I'd used since middle school. Tatsuya Kazehaya.

He glanced at the clipboard as he took it back, and something in his posture changed, quick but obvious, a flicker of recognition he didn't bother hiding. "Kazehaya," he said, quieter. "That name mean something to you?"

My hand tightened on my bag strap. *"Here we go."* "Depends what you think it means."

"Daichi Kazehaya." He set the clipboard down carefully, both hands flat on the counter. "He built those pods, back when Kazuhaya Tech was still around."

I nodded once, slowly. "He was my father."

His face softened, something almost apologetic under the recognition. "Leon," he said, offering a hand across the counter. "My sister and I run this place now. Your dad's work is a big reason we're still running at all. I'm sorry for your loss."

I shook it, firm and brief, and let go before my throat could do anything I wasn't going to deal with right now. "Thanks."

Leon nodded toward a back hallway, easy about it. "Pods are through there, second door on the left. Take your time. No one else is using them tonight."

• • •

The pod sat in the corner of a small, dim room that smelled like dust and old plastic. One overhead light caught the curved shell in long, uneven streaks where the casing had been scratched and resealed too many times. I stood in front of it a moment, taking in the worn edges and the faded Kazuhaya Tech logo near the base, a relic from the days when full-dive was still experimental and nobody knew if it would catch on.

*"Dad built this."* It sat heavier in my chest than I wanted, pride and grief tangled together, and I wasn't going to look at it right now. *"Alright. Let's see if I can make it work."*

I dropped my bag beside the pod and pulled the sleeve out. The access drive slid free, smaller than I'd expected, black with a single port and the HGO logo etched in thin gold lines that flashed when I turned it in the light. It felt expensive enough to make me nervous holding it.

The pod's access panel opened with a soft click on a mess of old connectors, nothing like the plug-and-play of the third-gen helmets, just a tangle of ports and cables that hadn't been touched in years, dust packed in the gaps. This was going to take a minute.

I opened the manual on the floor beside me. Dad's handwriting crowded the margins, notes from problems nobody else had bothered to solve, and I cross-referenced the diagrams against the panel with my hands already moving. The manual got me the bypass. It didn't get me everything. Whole sections of the harness were missing, pages he'd never written or someone had taken, and I wrote down the gaps in my head for later.

The override wasn't elegant. It forced the pod to accept input it hadn't been built to handle, rerouting power through secondary channels that weren't made for the load. I worked quickly, my hands steady even though my shoulders were wound tight, the same motions I'd practiced on the EB when it needed repair.

*"If this fries the drive, I'm done. I can't afford another one."*

The drive clicked into place with a sound that felt final, and the pod's internal display flickered on, text scrolling too fast to read, system checks running themselves. I snapped the panel shut and stood there with my hands tingling from the work. *"Moment of truth."*

I climbed in, the padding worn and compressed from years of use, and lay back as the shell hissed shut above me. The heads-up display lit inside my visor, white text on black. I told myself to slow down my breathing and let the numbers settle.

***[KAZUHAYA TECH FIRST-GENERATION VRPOD]***

***[INITIALIZING...]***

***[DRIVE DETECTED: HGO ACCESS LICENSE]***

***[WARNING: HARDWARE INCOMPATIBILITY DETECTED]***

***[OVERRIDE ENABLED]***

***[PROCEEDING...]***

The display went dark. Then the world did.

Sound came first, a low hum building underneath everything, bass-heavy, loud enough that I felt it in my teeth. Then light, bright enough that my eyes watered before the system adjusted. I was standing in a white void, no walls, no floor I could see, just empty space in every direction, the kind of nothing that should have been disorienting and wasn't.

*"Holy shit. It worked."* The grin tried to happen before I caught it. I was actually in.

Text floated at eye level, clean white letters with no shadows.

***[WELCOME TO ERYNDAL]***

***[CHARACTER CREATION WILL NOW BEGIN]***

A small bead of light drifted out of the white and settled in front of me, bobbing in place, and a bright, bubbly voice rang out of nowhere.

***[Hi there! I'm Lumi, your guide to Eryndal! Let's find the perfect you!]***

*"Another mascot. Of course."* I huffed out something close to a laugh and kept my eyes on the bead.

The bead bobbed higher, plainly pleased with itself, and kept talking.

***[First, a little about where you're going! Hero's Glory Online is a sandbox, so a lot of it is built by the people in it. Everything you earn stays with you, the people you meet remember what you do, and nothing resets when you log off.]***

I knew all of it. Taji had recited it at the breakfast table like scripture, and I'd tuned out every word. I shifted my weight on the nothing under my boots and let her keep going.

Lumi swirled in a lazy circle, trailing sparks, and her voice dropped like she was sharing a secret.

***[Eryndal is watched over by five patrons! Each one favors a people and gifts them something no one else gets. Seranis, goddess of creation, watches over the demi-humans. The others watch over the elves, the humans and the dwarves. And the fifth? Their gifts come with a price, so most adventurers keep their distance!]***

*"Five gods, and one of them's bad news. Eren would've had his pick memorized by now."* The old plastic of the pod squeaked against my jacket, and I decided I could care about it after I'd picked a face.

The bead bounced once, like the whole speech had been rehearsed for me alone.

***[Choose your race, and the patron who favors it will bless you. Ready? Let's begin!]***

A wheel formed in front of me, spinning slowly, each segment marked with a race name and a small icon. ***Human. Elf. Dwarf. Orc. Beastfolk. Goblin. Frostborn. Drakekin.*** The Goblin segment sat near the bottom, glowing a dull red next to the brighter colors, and even without reading the stats I could tell it was the lowest-tier option, the joke pick nobody took seriously.

I wasn't attached to any of them. Human asked the least of me, so my hand moved toward it, ready to lock it in and get on to attribute allocation, and then I hesitated, my fingers hovering a few inches from the wheel.

I looked down at the Goblin segment, that dull red glow, almost mocking next to everything else.

*"Just wanna see what it says."*

On impulse I couldn't have explained, I reached out and tapped it. A light touch, barely a press.

The wheel lurched to a stop.

***[Oh! W-wait, that segment isn't...]***

Lumi's voice tripped over itself and stretched thin, and the bead shuddered at the edge of my vision.

The lights flickered.

Not just in the pod, everywhere. The hum cut off mid-note, and the white void dissolved into a weightless black that made my stomach drop. My hand shot out for something solid that wasn't there, my body hunting for an up or a down that didn't exist anymore.

*"What the hell."*

Sound crackled back in, warped and wrong, and Leon's voice cut through the dark from outside the pod, muffled but clear.

"Hey, you alright in there?"

I blinked hard, but there was nothing to see except the afterimage of the white void. "Yeah," I called back, voice tight, my hands shaking. "I'm fine."

"Backup generator just kicked in," Leon said, closer now, right outside the shell. "System should stabilize in a second. You running anything unusual in there?"

*"Do I tell him? Does he even know about the override?"* The pod's external monitor had been dark when I walked in, a dead screen Leon had never bothered to fix because solo sessions went unmonitored, which meant he couldn't see anything that was happening in character creation. He didn't know, and he couldn't know unless I told him.

"No," I said, as level as I could make it. "Just character creation. Standard stuff."

"Alright." His boots scuffed away. "Let me know if it kicks again. Might be the age of the hardware, these things aren't built like they used to be."

The lights stabilized, and the hum came back lower and steadier. The white void flickered into place around me, walls resolving out of nothing, and the character-creation wheel reappeared, but it wasn't spinning anymore. The bead of light was gone. Whatever Lumi had been about to say, she wasn't there to say it.

The Goblin segment, the one that had been glowing dull red a moment ago, now burned bright green, pulsing faintly, locked in and waiting for me. Text appeared beneath it, clean and final.

***[RACE SELECTED: GOBLIN]***

***[Now, which of the five will you ch—]***

The line broke off mid-word, the letters fraying at the edge like a bad signal. I stared at the empty spot where Lumi's bead had hung.

***[PATRON ASSIGNED BY DEFAULT: SERANIS, GODDESS OF THE DEMI-HUMANS]***

*"I didn't pick a goddess either. Apparently the game picked one for me."*

***[PROCEEDING TO ATTRIBUTE ALLOCATION...]***

I stared at the words, my breath caught somewhere between my ribs and my throat.

*"No. No no no. That's not what I picked. I was just looking."* I reached for the wheel, trying to go back, trying to undo whatever the power blip had locked in, but the interface didn't respond. The wheel was gone. Only the confirmation screen remained, and under it, one button glowing soft white.

***[CONTINUE]***

*"This can't be real."* Even as I thought it, I knew it was.

My hands were shaking when I pressed **[CONTINUE]**, not because I wanted to, but because there was nothing else to press, no back button, no cancel, just forward into whatever this was about to become.

The screen dissolved, and a new set of numbers began to form in front of me, floating at eye level, waiting to tell me exactly how bad this was.

**[END CHAPTER THREE]**
