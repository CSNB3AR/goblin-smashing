# Chapter Three: Into the Game

I woke up wrong, sluggish and heavy, on the couch where I'd slept most nights since the funeral. Traffic noise leaked up from the street, a scooter whining past, a shutter rattling somewhere, and under it the fridge humming in the kitchen. Sunlight instead of the dim pre-dawn gray, warm on my eyelids in a way that said it was way past morning.

My phone was still in my hand, the screen dark and cool against my palm. I thumbed it awake: **3:04 PM**.

*"No. No, no, no."* I was off the couch before I was fully awake, the blanket sliding to the floor, my heart already going. *"The morning shift. I told Gato I'd cover it."* Extra hours, extra money, and I'd slept straight through it. *"Gato is going to kill me."*

A message from him sat at the top of the screen, sent before seven.

**[Opened up myself. Figured you'd be dead on your feet after the night shift. Don't sweat it, kid. Sleep. - Gato]**

I read it twice. *"Damn. He ain't tripping."* The panic drained out of me and I dropped onto the arm of the couch. A day off and a game waiting, but I couldn't grind that and keep picking up shifts. Sooner or later I'd have to call Gato and step back.

The television was still paused on the soldiers marching through the gate. Momo's door stood open, her room empty, the bed made lopsided the way she did it on her way out. Taji's side of the couch was cold.

A note sat on the kitchen counter, folded once, my name across the front in Chiyo's handwriting.

**[Left early with the twins for the Kyoto trip, didn't want to wake you after the night you had. We'll be back in six days. Please eat something real, not just convenience store rice balls. Chiyo]**

I smoothed the fold. *"The trip. Right."* It had been on the fridge calendar for two weeks and I'd still lost track. I opened the fridge, found the container of rice and grilled fish she'd left, and ate it standing at the counter, cold. Six days with nobody home but me.

My phone buzzed. A text from **Nexus Enterprises**.

**[Your HGO Access Drive order has been processed and is ready for pickup. Please collect within 24 hours of this notification. Pickup Code: 4472-KAZE]**

The sale clock had a fifth of its seventy-two hours gone. *"Okay. I need to go pick this up. Today."* I killed the television before I could look at it any longer. Corporate could run HGO on every screen at the store, but not in my living room.

I showered fast, hot water on my neck until the smell of the store was gone, then wrung out my locs and tied them up high, a few strands left loose along my face. In my room I pulled on my black and red Urawa Red Diamonds jersey, the club Dad always followed, with black joggers, my hoop in and the chain swinging, jacket over my arm.

Out in the living room I dropped onto the couch, hair still damp, and opened the HGO Companion App on my phone. WattsUp made every one of us put it on our phones in case a customer asked about the game, and I'd told myself that was the only reason. It wasn't. I hated how much of it I knew.

The feed opened on a headline. **[Season Zero final: London Spire, Guild Leader Edmund Vane, 10,800. Aogiri, Guild Leader Lin Haoyu, 10,250. Stillwater, Guild Leader Dosuke AI, fourth. Kansai Black Lotus, ninth.]** *"Fourth. Good for him."* Dosuke was the one player I'd actually sat and watched, and his build looked far too much like the one Eren and I used to draw on our tablets. *"Eren's guild got ninth. Bastard. Probably carried by Haruto."* If I was going to make anything in there, I'd have to be bigger than that, and it was going to be harder with every new player pouring in. I scratched at my goatee and tapped over to the market.

The auction board was packed. Unique legacy items from the closed beta sat at the top with millions on them, prices you read twice. Under them, new listings stacked up with every refresh, fresh gear and raw materials from players who'd started over a few hours ago, everybody back on the same line. A posting fee and a cut on every sale. *"That tax has to eat most of it before the seller sees a yen."*

*"Everything's new. Nobody's ahead."* The closed beta was over and the gates were open, and a whole wave of players was pouring in. I was part of that wave. *"I need to ride the new-game momentum. Get in early, get lucky, land something I can sell."* Crownfall's ten million was the only official prize pool, but the way this game was set up, maybe you could make money anywhere. It couldn't only be players coming in, either. With a market like that, businesses had to be too. I pulled up the wallet tab. **[HGO Coins: 1 = ¥4]** **[Withdraw / Deposit]** *"That doesn't look like play money."* I needed a balanced build, one that could scale across everything the game had, not just the first hour.

The race table sat on the same page. I opened the Elf row without really deciding to. Spellforger, Wisdom stacked high, the build I'd spent months drawing out with Eren and never once played. Dosuke ran a Spellforger of his own, a unique one. Eren's had hidden mechanics, mist and a few others, that you had to unlock and that, as far as I could tell, he never touched. Haruto had looked straight through him last night. *"I'd just like to see who the brain behind that build looks like when it's actually playing it."* Eren had the build. I was pretty sure he didn't know how to run it. And it was a closed-beta build. After a balance pass it might not even be playable, so I'd treat all of it as experimental, a new game: more players, new stuff, probably a new race, fixes.

I caught myself grinning at the screen. Half excitement, half nerves. Dad's game, the world he said he helped create, and at least I had an idea what I'd build instead of walking in blind. *"If I run into that son of a bitch in there, I can't be the one who gets outmatched."* It wasn't revenge. The last thing I wanted was to hand Eren any kind of recognition. Haruto's face crossed my mind with it.

*"Six days."* Chiyo would be back by then, and I wanted something of value to show her. *"I've never played a minute of it, only watched."* I couldn't let how I used to feel about this game decide how I went in. Clear head. My goal was to start making a positive income, for Mom, the twins, Chiyo. I was doing it for them. I was perched on the edge of the cushion, and I made myself lean back.

My bank balance:

**[¥47,320]**

Every month I paid Chiyo what she asked, groceries and a small bill. It was never the real number. I was pretty sure she knew I knew. It still felt good to put it in her hand.

I opened Loop, the big social app where everything happened, gaming included. I followed the HGO account, Yuna, a few major accounts like Dosuke AI and a few guilds, to keep up with updates.

Then the cafe booking app, to see if I could get in somewhere, since I didn't own a rig. Second-gen still meant full-body rigs. The third-gen helmets that came out with the beta ran HGO and the other VR games, full dive, a lot easier to keep at home, and they cost like a flagship phone, close to ninety thousand yen, which I didn't have. The sale and the open beta had every cafe in the city booked solid, third-gen waiting lists stretching into next year. Second-gen cafes ran pods built by developers the Tabuchis had put money into. The developers hadn't done anything to me, and I still wasn't putting a yen into anything the Tabuchis stood behind. The earliest opening was March anyway. *"Three months. Chiyo's not going to wait three months. She'll have me back at Tochi."*

I went down every list and every source. Nothing. Then, at the bottom of the third page, a gray tag: **[Legacy Hardware - First-Gen Pods Available]**. Sakura Arcade. Limited compatibility with newer titles. **[Free after 5 PM on weekdays.]**

First-gen. *"Dad."* That was all I thought for a second. I'd never seen his work out in the world. It could be an old place with a listing nobody had taken down, and the listing itself said it couldn't do much. *"This might not work at all."* But had anyone even tried? It was a gamble, and it was my only lead. I'd blow the whole thing up trying before I sat around for three months. I rubbed two fingers along my goatee while the worry settled in.

3:47 PM. The window opened in just over an hour, I still had to pick up the drive, and the EB had died on me last night. I laced my boots and went down the hall to my room. It was Dad's room first, the corner he'd worked out of, with the bed I hardly slept in, the smell of old solder and cold circuit boards hitting me at the door. His toolbox sat under the desk and his manual on the shelf above it. Chiyo had boxed most of his gear and carried it down to storage a few months back, I figured so the room could be mine again. *"I should clean this place up. Start working in here again."* It felt right standing in it, which surprised me. The funeral flashed up in my head, the closed casket, the quiet room, and the part of it that had never sat right with me. I shook it off and kept moving.

I wasn't leaving the manual on that shelf anymore. I pulled it down, the letter still hidden in the controller diagrams where I'd tucked it, and flipped to the electrical section until a note in his slanted handwriting stopped my thumb. **Aux cell sags under load. Jump at the marked terminals, then swap or recondition.** I put the manual and my laptop in my backpack, got it over both shoulders, picked up his toolbox by the handle, and rode the elevator down from the third floor.

The lobby of the Nakamura Grand was all pale stone and brass, a flower arrangement on the round table, the front desk empty, the bell untouched. There used to be a doorman and two clerks on a day like this. My phone buzzed. **[Chiyo: Hired someone to cover the desk. She'll be in later, so be nice and say hello.]** I got the storage key out of the drawer in her office desk and took the elevator down again, past the lobby to the basement level. The storage cage was lined with boxes labeled in Dad's blocky hand. I dug until I found one marked EB: a jump pack, cables, a spare 12-volt cell, a coil of wire, a handful of connectors. *"Everything he built could be fixed."*

Around back the air was cold and damp, a low haze sitting over the lot, my breath showing. The EB sat where I'd left it, black paint beaded with mist, dash dead. *"Please be the aux. If it's the big pack, I'm done."* I set up like a pit stop. A rag down on the concrete, Dad's toolbox open on the concrete with the trays fanned out in the order I'd reach for them, the meter in my left hand, the laptop on the seat with the lid up. It booted while I pulled the service cable and clicked it into the port, and the diagnostic window filled with cell voltages in a column. Traction pack, full. The 12-volt aux rail that fed the dash, the lock and the controller read six and a half volts, and when I put a load on it the whole column sagged. One cell fell faster than the rest, its curve bending early while the others held. I didn't need to test them one by one. *"That one."*

The EB was fully electric, the big pack ran the motor, but Dad never built anything without a 12-volt system, same as the Sedan, so any mechanic with a jump pack and cables could wake the controller. You can't jump a traction pack. You can jump the aux. I clamped the pack to the marked terminals, red to red, and watched the voltage climb on the screen until the controller woke and the dash flickered. I pulled the weak cell and checked it against the spare from the box. It dropped like a stone under load, bad internal resistance. I swapped in the spare, let the onboard charger top the rail up, and ran the load test again. The line stayed flat. Dad could have done all of it in his sleep. I wasn't there yet, but I could do a lot of what he did at my age, and that was how I'd gotten into Tochi on merit. Twenty minutes, and I didn't breathe easy until I pressed my thumb to the screen.

The dash took its half-second to read my print, then lit green, and the visor threw a route line across the street. *"Good. Good. We're good."* I let my forehead drop against the handlebar for one second. It ran better than it had in weeks, thanks to Dad's spares, but the box was a lot lighter than when I'd started. *"If I want to keep this thing alive past the winter, I'm going to need parts I can't pay for with forty-seven thousand yen."*

I clipped the manual into the rear case, pulled the helmet on, tucked my locs in so I could drop the visor, and rode out toward Ginza with the route line leading the way, the cold wind cutting straight through my jersey.

• • •

Nexus Enterprises' Ginza store sat between a watch dealer and a department store, glass and brushed steel.

I pushed through into cold, clean air that smelled of new plastic and floor polish.

I'd barely cleared the door when a voice carried across the showroom. A man at the counter had the same black case I'd come for gripped in one hand, shoppers staring. "I picked the wrong race, it was a thirty-second mistake. I want a refund."

"I'm sorry, sir. No refunds. Your only option is a respec, five hundred thousand yen, one time only."

"Five hundred thousand? I'm not paying that."

Two guards in tailored dark suits had already drifted in on either side of him, hands loose, looking like they'd done this before. They walked him toward the door while he kept talking, and the attendant pressed two fingers to his eyes. The showroom ran like a release night, a line out past the demo pods, cases clutched to chests, staff in Nexus polos moving fast. *"No refunds, and the respec is steep. So that's my one shot going in."*

Third-gen helmets sat on pedestals under warm spotlights, HGO's compass rose etched into the glass around each one. A wall-sized display looped last night's battle, Yuna's voice cutting through the room every time the reel restarted.

"...Thornwatch is London's, and London Spire takes Season Zero..."

*"Of course they're playing that on repeat."* I kept my head down and kept moving.

The pickup counter sat at the back, past a row of demo pods where kids waited their turn at five free minutes. The line moved slower than the clock on my phone could spare.

"Confirmation number," the attendant said, not looking up from his tablet.

I rattled it off from memory, 4472-KAZE, and he disappeared into the back and came out with the same sleeve he'd handed everyone else.

"Drive's pre-loaded with your license." He slid a printed waiver across the counter. "Same terms as everyone. One character, one account."

I signed without arguing, dropped the sleeve into my backpack and turned for the door before he could pitch me a warranty extension.

Outside, the light was going orange. 4:51 PM. Sakura Arcade's window opened in nine minutes, I still had to get across town, and the place might be an abandoned storefront with a listing nobody had taken down. *"If it's a dead end, I just burned the whole afternoon."* I got on the EB before I could talk myself out of it.

• • •

Sakura Arcade sat on a side street between a closed ramen shop and a convenience store hanging on by a thread. The building was older than the blocks around it, brick and wood paneling, warm pendant lights glowing through the front windows.

*"This is it? Doesn't look like much."* I parked the EB around the side, hung the helmet on one handlebar, and went to the door with my backpack on.

Inside, warm lantern lamps hung over brick pillars and steel beams, desks and monitors down one wall, and two tall dark pods standing in the open floor with cable bundles running up their sides and a blue glow in the seams. The rows ran back through what used to be three separate shops, the walls between them knocked out, and a staircase at the far end led down into rooms with no lights on.

Behind the counter, a guy in his mid-thirties looked up from a tablet, glasses sliding down his nose, dark hair pulled back in a small knot, an unlit cigarette tucked behind one ear and a smear of grease across his thumb, a faint smell of smoke and solder coming off him.

"Can I help you?" He sat up a little straighter. "Walk-ins are fine."

"I'm here for a pod," I said, stepping up. "First-gen, if you've got availability."

"I do." He said it evenly, like he'd said it before. "Before you get excited, they're first-gen Kazuhaya Tech. They don't play well with newer titles. I've turned away a dozen HGO hopefuls since the sale hit, and I'd hate for the free part to scare you off. If you test something newer on it and the pod dies, you're paying for the pod. It's the only moneymaker I've got."

"I know," I said, level, not defensive. "That's fine."

He studied me a second, then shrugged and slid a clipboard across with a pen. "Sign in. Name, time in. You've got the back room to yourself. Only one pod's actually functional, the other one's a shell."

I wrote my name across the top line in the same clean block letters I'd used since middle school. Tatsuya Kazehaya.

He read it as he took the clipboard back. "Kazehaya." He tapped the name with his pen. "That mean anything to you?"

Every time I'd said Dad's name, it had gotten me somewhere, and never anywhere good. His company had been dragged all over the news after the accident, every product pulled, his name stuck to the wreckage. *"Should I even say it?"* But this was a half-empty arcade and a man who looked like he'd heard worse. "Would you believe me," I said slowly, "if I said the man who made those pods was my dad?"

Leon stepped back from the counter. He looked at the name, then at me, then at the name again. "Damn. That would explain it." He waited. "Is that true?"

I nodded.

He nodded back, once, slow. "My days. I'm sorry, man." Then, dry, "Guess I didn't need to give you the speech about the pods." He held out a hand across the counter. "Leon. My sister and I run this place."

I shook it, firm and brief, and let go before my throat could do anything I wasn't going to deal with right now. "Thanks."

"The second pod's a shell," he said. "Second-gen runs off all-new parts, nobody touches third-gen hardware without a lawsuit, and first-gen parts dried up when Kazuhaya's stuff got pulled. I can't fix it."

Leon nodded toward a back hallway. "Pods are through there, second door on the left. Take your time. No one else is using them tonight."

• • •

The pod room was clean, the shell polished, the cable bundles tied off. Kazuhaya Tech was stamped in small letters near the base, and I stood there longer than I meant to. *"Dad really did this."* Pride and grief, both, and I wasn't going to look at either one right now.

I dropped my bag beside it and pulled the sleeve out. The drive was smaller than I'd expected, black with a single port and the HGO logo etched in thin gold. While the pod warmed up I flipped through the manual. Three sections tabbed in his blocky hand: the first-gen pod, the Sedan, the EB, every schematic and part number he'd ever pinned down. After the last tab the pages went blank, dozens of them. *"Room for my own notes."* I scrolled past the first-gen diagram and the build steps, every part laid out clean enough to copy. Second-gen pods had been glitching for years, and I figured that was half the reason the third-gen helmets blew up with HGO. *"Maybe that's why they wanted it so much."*

The drive slot beside the headrest was an old one, built for older drives, and the new drive went in with a shove that made me wince. I climbed in. The shell hissed shut.

The arcade went first, Leon's voice at the counter and the hum of the monitors thinning out until they were gone. Then the smell of cigarette smoke and dust and plastic. Then the padding against my back, the weight of my arms, the dry taste in my mouth, until I couldn't tell where my body stopped and the dark began. A drop, like missing a stair, and everything came back at once, cleaner than it had ever been.

Air first, cool and faintly sweet, tasting of rain. A breeze moved across my face from nowhere and lifted the loose strands of my locs. Under my boots was a floor I could feel, smooth and cold through the soles, and past it the white went on with a depth I could judge, near and far. I turned my hands over in front of me and every line in my palms was there. I could hear my own breathing, and under it a low, steady hum. *"Holy shit. It worked."* This wasn't a screen. I was standing in it. My shoulders dropped for the first time since I'd woken up, and the grin tried to happen before I caught it.

***[WELCOME TO ERYNDAL]***

A girl stepped out of the white, long white-silver hair falling to her waist, red eyes, a white dress with puffed sleeves, a small smile.

***[Hi there! I'm Lumi, your guide to Eryndal! Hero's Glory Online is a sandbox, so a lot of it is built by the people in it.]***

I knew all of it. Taji had recited it at the breakfast table like scripture, and I'd tuned out every word.

***[Eryndal is watched over by five patrons! Seranis, goddess of creation, watches over the goblins, the beastfolk and the drakekin. Sylthara watches over the elves, Aurenor the humans, and Khazren the dwarves and the frostborn. And Morvaine watches over anything wicked, and their gifts come with a price, so most adventurers keep their distance! Choose your race, and the patron who favors it will bless you.]***

*"Five gods, and one of them's bad news."*

A wheel opened around me, race names floating in the air at arm's length. ***Human. Elf. Dwarf. Orc. Beastfolk. Goblin. Frostborn. Drakekin.*** I reached for the Elf first and the stats unfolded beside it, Wisdom stacked high, exactly what I'd come for.

***[Elf! Graceful, gifted, and born to the old magic!]***

*"That's the one."* But I wanted to see them all, so I reached through the others one at a time, and each time Lumi said the name and started in on a line about the race. Human, balanced. Dwarf, sturdy. Orc, hard to put down. I read the numbers and let her talk, past Beastfolk, Frostborn, Drakekin, until Goblin was the last one left, the lowest numbers on the wheel. I didn't notice the shell warming against my back. I hadn't noticed anything real in a long time.

I reached for Goblin.

***[Goblin! The cunning little—]***

Her voice stretched thin and snapped. The light went out, and the floor was yanked out from under me.

I came back into my body all at once. The weight of it, the heat of the shell, the stink of hot plastic, my stomach rolling. I rolled onto my side and dry-heaved against the padding until it passed. The display was dead. I hit the power button. Nothing. Through the shell I could hear that the whole shop had gone dark, a chair scraping, Leon swearing near the counter.

"Hey," Leon called, closer, a flashlight beam crawling across the shell. "Whole place just lost power. You alright in there?"

"Yeah," I called back, voice rough. "I'm gonna step out for a sec, hit the restroom. Be right back."

"Take your time. I'll go flip the breaker."

I waited until his footsteps faded toward the front before I popped the shell and climbed out. Heat rolled off the casing. I dragged my bag over, plugged the laptop into the service port, and the screen lit my hands blue. The processor core read twenty percent and hot. The drive slot threw errors where I'd forced the new drive into the old fit. I laid Dad's schematic next to the readings and followed the lines. The core, basically a GPU the size of a brick, was why it was overheating, nearly dead and unable to hold what the game was asking of it. Then a slot built for the new drives, and some cheap wiring. I pulled up a third-gen core on my phone. **[PROCESSOR CORE, GEN 3: ¥420,000]** *"Damn."* The only place I knew that might have anything close was Tochi. I had no idea what it would take to get in the door, and I wasn't walking back into that school with my tail between my legs. But if Dad had left any kind of name there, maybe that was my best shot. I sat with that for a second, the heat off the casing against my face.

I couldn't fix any of it tonight. But the core still had a little energy in it, and that was enough. It was like jumping an old car. I could keep sparking it for as long as it held a charge, and when it finally died I'd have to replace it. The second pod was probably exactly that, a core already dead. I hardwired around the slot, stripped two leads, bridged the feed to the wall, and pressed the power. The shell lit. A second later the lights in the shop came back, and I laughed once under my breath. *"Lucky."* I let it charge for a few minutes, packed the tools back into the toolbox, and climbed in.

The shell hissed shut and the world came back in the same order, in reverse. The wheel hung where I'd left it, but it wasn't spinning and Lumi wasn't there.

***[RACE SELECTED: GOBLIN]***

***[Now, which of the five will you ch—]***

The line broke off mid-word, the letters fraying at the edge like a bad signal. I stared at the empty spot where she'd stood.

***[PATRON ASSIGNED BY DEFAULT: SERANIS, GODDESS OF THE DEMI-HUMANS]***

*"I didn't pick a goddess either. Apparently the game picked one for me."*

***[PROCEEDING TO ATTRIBUTE ALLOCATION...]***

*"No. No no no. That's not what I picked. I wanted the Elf."* I reached for the wheel, trying to go back, but the interface didn't respond. The wheel was gone. Only the confirmation screen remained, and under it, one button glowing soft white.

***[CONTINUE]***

*"This can't be real."* Even as I thought it, I knew it was.

I pressed **[CONTINUE]**, not because I wanted to, but because there was nothing else to press, no back button, no cancel, just forward into whatever this was about to become.

The screen dissolved, and a new set of numbers began to form in front of me, floating at eye level, waiting to tell me exactly how bad this was.

**[END CHAPTER THREE]**
