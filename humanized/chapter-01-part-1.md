# Chapter One: The Countdown Starts

Only me left on shift at WattsUp this late. I worked a rag along the counter in slow, even strokes over the same stretch of laminate, the rag dragging with a faint squeak, my shoulder stiff from the angle.

The counter had been clean for an hour already. Outside, the canopy lights hummed above the three empty charge bays, their cables dangling loosely from their enclosures, the tall pylon sign in the front yard cycling through ads against the totally black sky. Beyond the old gas pumps, dark at the opposite side of the parking lot, and beyond the row of small flags attached to the canopy's edge, Shibuya kept doing what Shibuya did at midnight; all of the signs were lit, and the crossing areas were still filled with people; a low thumping beat carried faintly across the parking lot.

An electric sedan had entered the center charge bay a little past 11 PM. The driver, a man wearing a wrinkled suit, was thumbing his phone while it charged with a soft double chime. He never came inside. Hardly anyone did anymore. The card readers in the bays did most of the work anyway. I couldn't remember the last time someone had fed gasoline into one of the ancient pumps, hoses coiled and dusty. And the motor cars were disappearing from Japan, just as vintage video games disappeared when Virtual Reality took over. WattsUp had quickly removed the old gas signs and called itself a charging chain. The last person I knew who kept feeding a pump was my dad. His hybrid still used gasoline, and the hand controls had worn smooth under his palms. I pushed that thought back where it lived and went back to the counter. I squinted up at the fluorescent lights buzzing overhead and rolled my neck until something popped.

The register drawer ticked in the quiet, a number I could have recited without looking. Gato was a good boss and the job was steady, and steady covered rent and groceries and not much else. I tried not to do the other math.

I caught myself in the dark half of the window between the counter and the door and looked longer than I meant to. Locs pulled back from my face with an elastic that had gone soft from reuse. A couple of stray locs fell free at my temple no matter how many times I tucked them back. The thin, pale scar on my forehead, directly above my right eyebrow, looked almost new even eleven years after the accident that caused it. I sported a thin goatee along my jawline. A Black face under the store lighting, my mother's dark brown skin gone sallow, the tired showing mostly around my eyes. I had my mother's height, six foot three of long legs and long arms, and my father's hands, steady and precise, the kind that fixed things instead of breaking them, and some nights working nights like this felt like its own kind of inheritance I wasn't sure I'd earned yet.

My cell phone vibrated loudly against the counter as Chiyo's name lit up the screen.

**[Tabuchi called again. Told them the usual. The lawyers are handling it, so don't worry if anything turns up at the hotel, I've got it. Also, Tochi's submission window closes on the 23rd and the entrance exams are Jan 4, I'm gonna need a response soon, Tatsu.]**

I read it twice, once for the words and once for what she hadn't bothered spelling out. Chiyo called it ordinary debt collection, same as always, and mentioned the lawyers like it was nothing. I'd stopped being sure it was ordinary a while ago. The demands kept circling back to my father's old paperwork specifically, in a way that never fully squared with "just pay us."

*"Dad wouldn't want her fighting this battle for him."* My chest tightened. *"This wasn't hers to carry. Never was."*

I typed back a thumbs-up emoji, because a thumbs-up cost nothing and didn't invite follow-up questions I didn't have answers for yet, and flipped my phone face-down on the counter.

The screen over the register ran what it always ran, HGO, and I hadn't picked it. All day it had sat at the edge of my eye, a bright rectangle I'd trained myself not to look at, the same logo, the same clips, the same voices selling the same thing. The WattsUp Corporation had purchased a sponsorship space within Hero's Glory Online, just as dozens of smaller companies were attempting to place their logos upon one of the largest titles currently available while it remained in closed beta, and the corporation's stock price had climbed with the game each week since then. Thus corporate had instructed each display in every location to continuously run it. Gato had stopped complaining about it a month ago. Most nights that meant some replay or guild footage playing quietly behind me while I worked, and I kept my back to it. I'd gotten good at that. Tonight corporate had turned the volume up on the whole chain for the last battle of the closed beta, the one they'd been pushing all week, and it was getting harder to talk myself out of listening.

Then Yuna's bright, familiar voice rose over the loop, riding the crowd noise, calling the fight, loud enough that I quit pretending I wasn't listening. My rag stopped mid-stroke.

"Aogiri is trapped inside that fortress," Yuna declared, and she raised her pitch slightly as she rode up into the roar of the crowd. "And the Knights of the London Spire will not relent! To everyone just joining in, I am Yuna, and this is Season Zero's last battle! Live from Singapore! The Knights possess 10,500 season points, Aogiri trails by 250, and winning this battle gives possession of Thornwatch for 300 points! Thornwatch is Lunarest's newest zone in Beta, an old fortress where the king once dwelled, abandoned and destroyed in Lunarest's uncharted north, and any guild may attempt to claim ownership. In order to reach Thornwatch you must navigate Thornwatch Forest, and whatever resides in Thornwatch Forest is far worse than any guild. Considering how close we are to the end of this season, if either team loses this battle they lose everything, with only two hours remaining on their countdown timer. Aogiri must win this battle, and the Knights merely have to survive, under a seventy-two hour death lock which eliminates anyone who dies during this battle for good. Three days have worn this battle down to twenty Knights versus twelve Aogiri!"

"And that is what makes Hero's Glory Online unlike every other MMORPG out there!" Yuna continued as the crowd swelled beneath her voice. "Hero's Glory Online is an open-world Sandbox VRMMORPG, so many parts of it are created by its players. All NPC characters are living, all quests and raids continue indefinitely, and this is still only our closed beta period, so there is so much more to come! This is also true of your route, though a path like mine is extremely unique."

"But look who they're fighting for!" Yuna exclaimed as the crowd roared louder beneath her voice. "The Knights fight for Prince Dorrin's banner, Aogiri fights for Prince Maldren's banner, and Prince Ilyan is absent from this battle in Lunarest City. If you've lost track of your storyline due to becoming too deeply entrenched in your grind, here is a brief summary of events leading up to today. I've seen and visited many taverns, and with the king ill, the princes moved in separate directions. That's why they employed many members from various guilds for their respective causes. Tonight marks the first occasion those guilds have clashed head-to-head over territorial control, with a flag awaiting planting and with neither prince present, near the edge of Lunarest's map where those trailers hinted at further regions! It is the biggest world quest anyone has cracked open so far, nobody knows where it ends, and tonight it decides which team wins Season Zero! Thanks again HGO for allowing me to host tonight's event! Thanks again to every fan who has listened to my songs this far! New song coming very soon!"

That was a lot of information for midnight. I caught myself a step closer to the screen than I'd meant to be, rolled one shoulder until my neck popped, and walked back to the counter with the rag hanging from my hand. *"Princes and banners. Not my problem."* My eyes tracked back to the screen before I'd decided to look.
